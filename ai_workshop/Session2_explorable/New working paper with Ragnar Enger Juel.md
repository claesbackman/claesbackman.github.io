New working paper with Ragnar Enger Juelsrud and Ella Getz Wold, "Mortgage Constraints: Household Responses, Market Adjustment and Financial Stability." 

Many countries have introduced policies that limit mortgage borrowing, with the aim of reducing credit growth and improving financial stability. We refer to these policies as "Mortgage constraints", but they are also often called borrower-based macroprudential policies. These policies tend to reduce mortgage borrowing, which may improve financial stability, but they also potentially come with costs for the borrowers that are affected, and may indirectly affect others through house prices, rents or other types of spillovers. 

We survey the evidence on these policies, organized around a simple framework that splits the effect of a tighter mortgage constraint into three parts. Each part draws on a different body of evidence: household-level studies, research on housing markets and lenders, cross-country studies of aggregate credit, and the literature linking credit booms to financial crises. 

The direct effect of mortgage constraints falls on households whose borrowing is limited. These households are typically younger borrowers with lower wealth or income, where the reduction in borrowing comes from lower savings or family, and from byuing cheaper properties. A sizable share of the lower borrowing also reflects households abstaining from a purchase, either remaining renters or staying in their current property. That credit declines after these policies is thus a poor guide to the private burden. 

 The second effect comes from spillovers to house prices, rents and credit supply caused by the lower borrowing by exposed households, which affects households who are not directly facing these new constraints. We think these spillovers are potentially large, but also caution that we have only a limited number of studies who actually estimate them. The appendix provides a brief guide that we hope is useful for researchers working in this area. 

The third effect is the corrective benefit of lower financial vulnerability. This evidence is more indirect, but some micro-level evidence suggest that mortgage constraints improve resilience, where others caution that lower leverage comes with smaller liquid buffers that can leave households less able to smooth consumption after job loss

Three findings stand out. The direct cost falls mainly on young and low-wealth households, largely because fewer mortgages are originated. Aggregate credit responds several times more than household-level estimates imply when added up, which is consistent with general equilibrium amplification, although the studies differ in setting and outcome. The corrective benefit is the least understood part: of the 154 tightening episodes we examine, only 10 occurred when household credit and house prices were both growing fast, the conditions under which crisis risk has historically been highest. That pattern is consistent both with policy arriving late and with policy keeping economies out of the danger zone, and telling the two apart is one of the open questions we highlight.
