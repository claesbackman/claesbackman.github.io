8Conclusion

This paper asks whether AI models tailor financial advice to the country of the investor who asks. In a pre-registered experiment, I pose three financial questions to thirteen frontier AI models in twenty-one countries, decompose any country effect into language, country-naming, and local-framing components, and compare the advice with a country-specific normative benchmark. The main result is that the models localize some decisions and not others. On the allocation question the advice is compressed into a twelve-point band of country means, with the U.S. baseline near its top. Every country contrast that survives correction is negative and modest. Which model a household consults matters an order of magnitude more than which country it consults from. That ordering is specific to the allocation question, and on the mortgage question it reverses. There the same models recommend investing 10 to 29 percentage points less than in the United States in every country where the prompted rate is realistic. On the inflation question they switch from equities to inflation-linked bonds in Brazil, Turkey, and Mexico. Nearly every model shares the direction of these differences.

The cross-country pattern in the allocation advice matches neither the measured risk preferences and financial literacy of the populations the models advise nor any version of a standard portfolio benchmark. With unhedged currency risk, the benchmark prescribes a thirty-two-point spread across countries against the twelve points the advice spans, and on the mortgage question, in an exploratory comparison, the models recommend investing 13 to 36 points more than the question’s own benchmark prescribes in all sixteen calibration countries. What moves the allocation advice instead is what the prompt says about the individual investor, and specifically the cues a retail risk questionnaire collects. Stated risk tolerance raises the recommended equity share by 46 percentage points, and a twenty-year rather than one-year horizon raises it by 40, although the horizon is irrelevant under CRRA preferences with i.i.d. returns. A stated generous pension barely registers. This joint pattern, a large horizon response beside a near-absent pension response, identifies the rule as the “popular” financial advice of Canner et al. (1997) and Choi (2022) rather than a portfolio rule over the household’s balance sheet. Exploratory probes show that the models do register the balance sheet, but only in one direction. The advice de-risks sharply when a stated cue raises the investor’s exposure, as with a pension invested in stocks or a stake equal to all of her liquid wealth. It stays nearly flat on the mirror-image cues that should raise the risky share, such as a guaranteed annuity, a currency hedge, or a stated cash cushion. A retail suitability assessment produces the same one-sided pattern, because it screens the risk a client is willing to take against her capacity to bear losses, so a stated hazard lowers the recommendation while stated safety does not raise it on its own.

The compression is not a failure to tell the countries apart. Local financial context appears in three quarters of non-U.S. explanations without prompting and in almost all of them once the prompt names local instruments, and the same country regression that finds effects of at most 11 points on the equity share finds effects of up to 78 points on whether the explanation names that context. The allocation, by contrast, barely responds to localization cues. Naming local instruments moves the recommended share by less than a point, and asking in the local language rather than in English moves it by 1.3 points, so the country name itself carries most of each country effect. The models retrieve what distinguishes the investor’s country and leave it out of the number. What the number does carry are the conventions of U.S.-English retail advice. The modal recommendation is the 60/40 balanced portfolio, the United States sits near the top of the band, and explicit rules of thumb appear in the explanations of the English-prompting countries and almost nowhere else. The small language effect is consistent with interpretability evidence that multilingual models solve tasks in a shared, English-leaning concept space and confine language-specific processing to the input and output layers.

I note four limitations. First, I do not observe the model internals, so the reading that translation leaves the underlying policy intact is an inference from behavior rather than a direct measurement. Second, the normative benchmark is deliberately static and closed form. A country-calibrated life-cycle model in the tradition of Cocco et al. (2005) would add realism, but the one existing such treatment reaches the same 100 percent corner as the balance-sheet benchmark here (Choukhmane et al. 2026), so it is unlikely to overturn the balance-sheet logic. Third, the experiment queries each model through its API in a single turn with no system prompt. Consumer chat applications add system prompts, memory, and web search, which may shift the level of the advice. Every comparison in the paper holds the interface fixed across countries and investors, so the contrasts, the variance ordering, and the advice rule are the findings most likely to carry over, and the levels are the most likely to differ. The elicitation probe of Section 4.6 shows what the models would ask in a second turn, and it is investor-specific rather than country-specific, so the single-turn format is not what produces the cross-country compression. Fourth, the main estimates are a snapshot of a fast-moving panel, collected within a two-day window. A re-collection eleven weeks later shows that the model behind an unchanged identifier can move by as much as a country contrast (Appendix D). It also shows that a panel that had largely turned over gives advice with a similar structure, with model identity the largest source of variance and most countries below each model’s U.S. cell, although one round of turnover cannot establish a trend. Two questions remain open: whether the compression narrows as models improve, and whether retrieval or fine-tuning can induce localization without reintroducing the language-keyed inconsistency that the shared concept space currently avoids.

These findings speak to the market structure that AI advice creates. Regulation, the trust a credence good requires, and language once kept financial advice national. A handful of foundation models now supply it in any language and at almost no marginal cost, outside the regulatory perimeter that binds human advisors. They answer the allocation question with nearly one policy everywhere, while their advice on debt and on inflation protection differs across countries. A global market for advice makes it possible to ask how much geographic differentiation the economics actually warrants, and the answer differs by decision. On the risky share, little differentiation is warranted, since a currency-hedged world fund prescribes the same share everywhere, and the country differences the models do produce follow the country name, not the fundamentals the benchmark prices. On the mortgage, where the local real rate decides the answer, large differences are warranted, and the models differentiate in the warranted direction. Because production is concentrated in a few models trained on the same corpus, any error in that policy is common to all the households exposed to it, whereas the fragmented industry of human advisors would have diluted it. The clearest such error is the one-sided treatment of the balance sheet, which cuts risk on stated exposure but barely responds to stated safe wealth. Suitability and best-interest rules oblige a human advisor to assess the client’s whole financial situation. The models apply only the protective half of that assessment, and they do so outside the perimeter where those rules bind. Whether the regulatory perimeter should extend to advice of this kind is the policy question this market structure poses.

The answers also do not show the investor where the advice adapts to her country. An investor who asks the same models about her portfolio allocation and about prepaying her mortgage receives advice that barely responds to her country in the first case and varies with it in the second. Without expertise of her own, she cannot tell from the answers which case she is in. Outside the United States the models name local instruments, taxes, and pension systems in most of their explanations of the allocation, so the part of the answer that visibly adapts to her country is the explanation she can read rather than the allocation that determines what she earns.
