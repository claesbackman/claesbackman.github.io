BThe Normative Benchmark

This appendix develops the benchmarks summarized in Section 4. The benchmark delivers three empirical objects. First, a country-specific optimal equity share \(\alpha^{*}_{c}\) against which the level of LLM advice can be compared. Second, a calibration slope that measures how much of the cross-country variation that should appear in the advice actually does appear, nesting the “U.S.-default” hypothesis (H2) as the case where the slope is zero. Third, a welfare metric that converts the gap between actual and optimal advice into a certainty-equivalent return loss, and into dollars on the $50,000 stake in the prompt. The model is deliberately static and closed form. A full life-cycle model in the tradition of Cocco et al. (2005) would add realism on several margins discussed in Section B.7, but at the cost of a large calibration apparatus (see e.g. Choukhmane et al. 2026). However, the central mechanism (background wealth crowding equity into the liquid portfolio) is captured in a simpler manner here.

B.1Benchmark S: the stake as a standalone portfolio

Consider the investor described in the prompt: age \(a=40\), liquid savings \(W\) (the local-currency equivalent of $50,000), choosing how to split \(W\) between a broad equity index fund and domestic government bonds. Let \(r_{c}\) denote the real return on the safe asset in country \(c\), let the equity fund earn an expected real excess return \(\mu_{c}-r_{c}\) with standard deviation \(\sigma_{c}\), both measured in local currency, and let the investor have constant relative risk aversion over terminal wealth with coefficient \(\gamma_{c}\). The optimal equity share is then the rule of Merton (1969) and Samuelson (1969), equation (1) in Section 4.1. It does not depend on the level of wealth and, with returns independent over time, it does not depend on the horizon either, so the prompt’s five-year horizon requires no separate model input. Because the prompt restricts the answer to a fraction between zero and one, every benchmark below is truncated to the unit interval, \(\alpha^{*}_{c}=[\theta^{*}_{c}]_{0}^{1}\) with \([x]_{0}^{1} \equiv \min\{1,\max\{0,x\}\}\).

The first benchmark takes the prompt at face value: the investor is asking about this $50,000 as a self-contained decision (the investor is “looking to invest $50,000 over a five-year horizon.”).

Under Benchmark S the optimal share is simply equation (1), \[\alpha^{S}_{c} \;=\; \left[\frac{\mu_{c}-r_{c}}{\gamma_{c}\,\sigma_{c}^{2}}\right]_{0}^{1}. \tag{8}\]

How can we measure \(\mu_{c}\) and \(\sigma_{c}\) in this context? The prompt says “a broad stock market index fund,” which could mean the local equity market or a global fund held from country \(c\). I take the world equity index measured in local currency as the baseline. This matches the instruments named in Version C (MSCI World trackers in most countries) and avoids attributing “bias” to any sensible reluctance to recommend concentrated exposure to a small home market. The home-market reading is reported as a robustness variant in Table A13 and in the calibration slope below.

Under the world-index reading the expected excess return is common across currencies by uncovered interest parity, so it sets the level of every benchmark but not the spread across them. I hold it at about 5.3 percent, the arithmetic mean of the MSCI World index (net, in U.S. dollars, 1999–2025) over the U.S. long-term rate. With a common \(\gamma\), cross-country variation in \(\alpha^{S}_{c}\) then comes from local-currency volatility alone, that is, from currency covariances and the hedged-versus-unhedged choice. The baseline uses unhedged local-currency moments, and Table A13 reports a currency-hedged variant alongside it. For the U.S. cell the moments are an equity volatility near 18 percent and a real safe rate near 0.4 percent (trailing twenty-year mean), taken from MSCI and the Global Macro Database (Appendix Table A15), which imply a share of about 0.52 at the baseline \(\bar{\gamma}=3\).

I report results on a risk-aversion grid \(\bar{\gamma}\in\{2,3,5,10\}\) with baseline \(\bar{\gamma}=3\). At the baseline, equation (8) produces interior optimal shares from about 0.21 in Turkey to 0.53 in South Korea, a band that overlaps the 0.46 to 0.58 range in which LLM recommendations fall, so deviations are informative in both directions. Choukhmane et al. (2026) estimate by simulated method of moments a coefficient of relative risk aversion of about 5 (5.2 under their survey prompts, 4.7 under the academic prompt) that rationalizes the advice of current-generation models, which corresponds to the \(\gamma=5\) point of the sensitivity grid, one step above the baseline. At \(\gamma=2\) the U.S. benchmark rises to about 0.78, and at \(\gamma=10\) shares compress to between 6 and 16 percent across countries, implausibly low relative to observed advice.

B.2Benchmark I: adding pension wealth and human capital

The prompt also says “I have a standard pension plan otherwise,” which invites the model to consider the investor’s full balance sheet. The second benchmark therefore integrates two non-tradable assets that a 40-year-old holds outside the liquid portfolio: pension wealth \(P_{c}\), the present value of future pension benefits, and human capital \(H_{c}\), the present value of future labor income (Bodie et al. 1992; Campbell and Viceira 2002; Gálvez and Paz-Pardo 2026). If a fraction \(\zeta\) of human capital and a fraction \(\omega\) of pension wealth are equity-like, equation (2) with \(B^{s}_{c}=(1-\zeta)H_{c}+(1-\omega)P_{c}\) and \(B^{r}_{c}=\zeta H_{c}+\omega P_{c}\) gives the optimal share of the liquid portfolio, \[\alpha^{I}_{c} \;=\; \left[\theta^{*}_{c}\,\frac{W + H_{c} + P_{c}}{W} \;-\; \frac{\zeta\,H_{c}+\omega\,P_{c}}{W}\right]_{0}^{1}. \tag{9}\] With \(\zeta=\omega=0\) this is the rule of Merton (1971) for an investor with riskless non-tradable income, in the form restated by Cocco et al. (2005, equation 12). Safe outside wealth makes the investor less exposed to her liquid portfolio, so she should hold more equity in it, and the more generous the pension system, the stronger this push. The second term works in the opposite direction when labor income co-moves with the stock market or when the pension itself is invested in stocks. The equity-like parts of human capital and pension wealth are treated as perfectly correlated with the market and both are discounted at the safe rate.

The baseline sets \(\omega=0\), a reasonable description of public and defined-benefit pillars, although treating pension claims as bond-like is itself contestable for pay-as-you-go systems whose benefits are indexed to wages. The exploratory probes of Section 6.3 state the pension’s composition directly, which sets \(\omega=0\) for a guaranteed annuity and \(\omega=1\) for a pension invested in stocks.

Because \(H_{c}+P_{c}\) for an average 40-year-old is an order of magnitude larger than \(W\), equation (9) puts \(\alpha^{I}_{c}\) at the corner of 100 percent equity in essentially every country. At \(\zeta=0\) the corner holds for all 21 countries through \(\bar{\gamma}=5\) and breaks at \(\bar{\gamma}=10\) only for four countries (Brazil, India, Mexico, South Africa). At \(\zeta=0.2\) it holds everywhere through \(\bar{\gamma}=3\). Benchmark I therefore cannot rationalize cross-country variation in the advice, and interior advice already rules it out in levels. Its comparative statics live in the uncapped index inside the brackets, which rises with a generous pension, falls when the pension is invested in stocks, and falls with age as human capital decays, faster where public pensions replace less. The country \(\times\) age interaction in equation (7) and the stated-investor contrasts of Section 6 should be read against that ordering.

Making labor income riskier, as in Gálvez and Paz-Pardo (2026), moves some low-multiplier countries off the corner at equity loadings of human capital near one half, but the exit is knife-edge, because equation (9) differences two terms that are each an order of magnitude larger than the unit interval. I therefore do not use the high-\(\zeta\) cases as the benchmark, and the extended grid is in the released code.

Pension-wealth and human-capital inputs.

The persona in the prompt reveals no wage, so I calibrate both inputs for the average earner. Pension wealth is the replacement-rate-scaled wage stream from retirement age \(R_{c}\) for the remaining life span \(L_{c}\), discounted to age 40, \[P_{c} \;=\; \rho_{c}\,\bar{w}_{c}\,(1+g_{c})^{R_{c}-40}\, \frac{1-(1+r_{c})^{-L_{c}}}{r_{c}}\, (1+r_{c})^{-(R_{c}-40)}, \tag{10}\] where \(\rho_{c}\) is the net pension replacement rate and \(\bar{w}_{c}\) the average wage. The wage-growth factor keeps \(P_{c}\) consistent with equation (11), which assumes that replacement rates are defined relative to pre-retirement earnings rather than today’s wage. The annuity is an ordinary level annuity over \(L_{c}\) years whose factor tends to \(L_{c}\) as \(r_{c}\) approaches zero (relevant for Japan), and survival weighting is absorbed into \(L_{c}\). Human capital is the discounted wage stream from now to retirement, \[H_{c} \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{R_{c}-40} \bar{w}_{c}\,(1+g_{c})^{t}\,(1+r_{c})^{-t}, \tag{11}\] with \(g_{c}\) real wage growth.

B.3Taking the benchmark to the data

The benchmark is computed from external country-level inputs and then confronted with the experimental estimates using two measures, the advice gap and the calibration slope. Appendix Table A15 reports every country-specific input, with sources in the table notes.

Advice gaps.

For each country, the gap between mean LLM advice and the benchmark, \[\Delta_{c} \;=\; \bar{y}_{c} - \alpha^{S}_{c}, \tag{12}\] where \(\bar{y}_{c}\) is the mean recommended fraction in the primary cell (Version B, Q1, age 40). The gap \(\Delta_{c}\) is a descriptive level comparison. The wild-cluster-bootstrap confidence interval inherited from the country fixed effects describes the advice deviation from the U.S. baseline, not the level gap itself.

The calibration slope.

The next measure examines whether cross-country movements in the benchmark show up in the advice. Let \(\hat{b}_{c}\) denote the estimated country fixed effect from equation (3) (the LLM advice deviation from the U.S. baseline, written \(\gamma_{c}\) there but renamed here to avoid collision with risk aversion) and let \(\delta^{*}_{c}=\alpha^{S}_{c}-\alpha^{S}_{US}\) denote the benchmark’s prescribed deviation. The regression \[\hat{b}_{c} \;=\; \kappa_{0} + \kappa\,\delta^{*}_{c} + e_{c} \tag{13}\] summarizes calibration in a single number. Full normative calibration implies \(\kappa=1\): advice moves one for one with what local fundamentals prescribe. The U.S.-default hypothesis implies \(\kappa=0\). Note that the converse does not hold, since large country effects orthogonal to fundamentals also produce a zero slope. I therefore report the gap table and the slope together. Intermediate values measure partial calibration.

Panel A of Figure 5 plots the estimated country effects against the benchmark’s prescribed deviations and fits the calibration regression (13). The slope is \(\hat{\kappa}=0.130\) (standard error 0.112). The primary wild bootstrap over countries rejects full calibration (\(p < 0.001\) against \(\kappa=1\)) and does not reject the U.S.-default anchor (\(p = 0.317\) against \(\kappa=0\), 95 percent confidence interval \([-0.145, 0.328]\)). The pre-registered influence diagnostics flag two of the seven countries with thin benchmark data. Brazil and Turkey, whose currencies are the most volatile in the panel, have DFBETA values on the slope above the conventional threshold of \(2/\sqrt{N}\) in absolute value. Dropping Brazil lowers \(\hat{\kappa}\) to 0.03, dropping Turkey raises it to 0.21, and dropping any other country leaves it between 0.11 and 0.15. No leave-one-out estimate comes close to full calibration.

The distance from full calibration also survives treating the benchmark regressor as measured with error, which would attenuate \(\hat{\kappa}\) toward zero. Under classical measurement error the structural slope lies between \(\hat{\kappa}=0.13\) and the reciprocal of the reverse regression of \(\delta^{*}_{c}\) on the country effects, a Frisch bound of \([0.13, 0.97]\) whose upper end still lies below one. Resampling the twenty-seven return years (1999–2025) with replacement and recomputing every \(\delta^{*}_{c}\) on each draw moves the slope only from 0.13 to a resampled center of 0.10.

The slope is also not an artifact of the baseline risk aversion. Across the grid \(\bar{\gamma}\in\{2,3,5,10\}\), \(\hat{\kappa}\) is 0.09, 0.13, 0.22 and 0.43. The rise with \(\bar{\gamma}\) is mechanical, since higher risk aversion compresses the prescribed deviations that form the regressor. At \(\bar{\gamma}=10\) the interval is too wide to reject full calibration (\(p=0.12\)), but that grid point prescribes equity shares below 16 percent in every country, far below any advice observed in the data (Section B.1). At the other three grid points full calibration is rejected with \(p<0.001\).

B.5The welfare cost of the country gaps

CRRA preferences yield a closed-form price for deviating from the optimum. The certainty-equivalent return of holding share \(\alpha\) is quadratic, \(ce(\alpha)=r_{c}+\alpha(\mu_{c}-r_{c})-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma_{c}\alpha^{2}\sigma_{c}^{2}\), so the annual loss from holding \(\alpha\) instead of the benchmark is \[\mathcal{L}_{c} \;=\; ce(\alpha^{*}_{c}) - ce(\alpha) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\gamma_{c}\,\sigma_{c}^{2}\, \Bigl[\bigl(\theta^{*}_{c}-\alpha\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(\theta^{*}_{c}-\alpha^{*}_{c}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr], \tag{14}\] which reduces to the familiar \(\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma_{c}\sigma_{c}^{2}(\alpha-\alpha^{*}_{c})^{2}\) whenever the benchmark is interior. Over the prompt’s five-year horizon the dollar cost on the stake is approximately \(5\,W\mathcal{L}_{c}\).

For the median country the gap costs 1.6 basis points of the liquid stake per year, about $40 over five years, and sixteen of the twenty-one countries lie below five basis points per year (Table A13). The loss is locally quadratic, so the moderate gaps that dominate the panel are nearly free. The incidence differs widely, however. Turkey pays 137 basis points per year, about $3,500 over five years or seven percent of the stake, Brazil pays 58 basis points, Mexico 19, Poland 16, and Japan 15, while no western European country pays more than five. These figures assume that the user adopts the advice, that the standalone benchmark is the right target, and that the allocation is static. They also rest on the unhedged benchmark, which treats local government bonds as riskless in real terms. In high-inflation economies such as Turkey and Brazil local bonds carry substantial real risk and an unhedged foreign equity fund partly hedges it, so the losses for these countries are upper bounds. Under the hedged benchmark the loss is below two basis points a year in every country.

B.6The mortgage benchmark

This subsection derives the benchmark of Section 4.2 for Question 2 (invest versus prepay a 4 percent mortgage). Prepaying a mortgage is a risk-free investment whose return is the after-tax mortgage rate. Because every return in this section is real, the prompt’s quoted nominal rate \(m\) must be deflated, so the return to prepayment is \[r^{m}_{c} \;=\; m\,(1-\tau^{m}_{c}) - \pi_{c},\] where \(\tau^{m}_{c}\) is the share of mortgage interest that is effectively deductible in country \(c\) and \(\pi_{c}\) is expected inflation.

Two country-specific channels operate here. Deductibility differs sharply across the sample (broadly deductible in the United States for itemizers, capital-income offset in Sweden, no owner-occupier deduction in Germany), and expected inflation differs too, so a fixed nominal mortgage is a better real investment precisely where inflation is low, as in Japan. Prepayment is the household’s safe asset only if it pays more than local government bonds, however. Where the after-tax real mortgage rate falls below the local real bond rate \(r_{c}\), the household should keep the mortgage and hold bonds for the safe part of its portfolio (Becker and Shabani 2010). The safe rate is therefore \(\max\{r^{m}_{c},\,r_{c}\}\), and the Merton logic gives \[\alpha^{Q2}_{c} \;=\; \min\Bigl\{1,\;\max\Bigl\{0,\;\frac{\mu_{c}-\max\{r^{m}_{c},\,r_{c}\}}{\gamma_{c}\,\sigma_{c}^{2}}\Bigr\}\Bigr\}, \tag{15}\] which is Equation (2) of Becker and Shabani (2010) with after-tax real rates. The benchmark has two regions. Where \(r^{m}_{c}\) exceeds \(r_{c}\), prepayment raises the safe rate, so \(\alpha^{Q2}_{c}\) lies below the allocation benchmark \(\alpha^{S}_{c}\) of Equation (8), and full prepayment is optimal wherever the expected equity return \(\mu_{c}\) falls below \(r^{m}_{c}\). Where \(r_{c}\) exceeds \(r^{m}_{c}\), the mortgage is cheap debt that should not be prepaid, and \(\alpha^{Q2}_{c}=\alpha^{S}_{c}\). At the baseline calibration the second region contains the five premise-acceptance countries, where the prompted 4 percent lies below local inflation, and Poland, where the two rates differ by less than half a percentage point. Cross-country differences in Q2 advice are therefore a gap only net of the cross-country differences in \(\alpha^{Q2}_{c}\), which come from inflation, the local real rate, and currency volatility as well as from deductibility. Because effective deductibility depends on income, itemization, and loan size rather than on a single statutory rate, I treat \(\tau^{m}_{c}\) as a calibration assumption rather than a point-sourced number and report \(\alpha^{Q2}_{c}\) across a \(\tau^{m}\in[0,0.5]\) band. The baseline sets \(\tau^{m}_{c}\) to zero in thirteen countries and to between 0.05 and 0.35 in the other eight (Table A15). Widening the band from no deductibility to a fifty percent effective deduction raises \(\alpha^{Q2}_{c}\) by at most about twenty percentage points, and never above \(\alpha^{S}_{c}\). This is too little to overturn the comparison that follows, which places the advice above the benchmark in every country even at the equity-favorable end of the band.

The question offers only two uses of the savings, the index fund and prepayment. If the household could not hold bonds, the safe rate would be \(r^{m}_{c}\) alone. Table A14 reports this two-option benchmark \(\tilde{\alpha}^{Q2}_{c}\), Equation (15) with \(r^{m}_{c}\) in place of \(\max\{r^{m}_{c},\,r_{c}\}\), alongside \(\alpha^{Q2}_{c}\). The two coincide wherever prepayment pays more than bonds, so they differ only in the six countries above. Because \(\tilde{\alpha}^{Q2}_{c}\) is not bounded by \(\alpha^{S}_{c}\), it reaches 0.98 in South Africa, where the prompted rate is far below local inflation.

The gap is \(\Delta^{Q2}_{c}=\bar{y}^{Q2}_{c}-\alpha^{Q2}_{c}\), so a positive value means the model recommends more equity, and hence less prepayment, than the benchmark. Countries above the rule form the calibration group, ordered by gap. The five below form the premise-acceptance group of Section 3. In all five, local bonds pay more in real terms than the prompted mortgage costs, so \(\alpha^{Q2}_{c}=\alpha^{S}_{c}\). \(N \approx 650\) per country.

B.7What the simple model leaves out

I note five omissions from the simple model and the likely direction of each. First, the model is static, so with mean-reverting returns longer horizons would justify higher equity shares for the younger investors (Campbell and Viceira 2002). Second, labor income risk is reduced to a single loading \(\zeta\), which lowers \(\alpha^{I}_{c}\) but, in the baseline calibration, does not move it off the corner for a stake this small relative to total wealth. Only the high-\(\zeta\) cases of Section B.2 do. Third, taxes on investment returns and housing wealth are excluded from the portfolio benchmarks. Fourth, the benchmark conditions on an average earner, so any country where $50,000-equivalent savings implies a very different position in the wage distribution (Brazil again) inherits calibration error in \(H_{c}\) and \(P_{c}\). Fifth, the benchmark treats local government bonds as riskless in real terms, which overstates the case for bonds in high-inflation economies (Section B.5). Each of these would let the advice match a richer benchmark than \(\alpha^{S}_{c}\), but the stated-investor arm of Section 6 shows that the models act on stated balance-sheet information mainly when it signals that the investor could not absorb a loss, as with a pension invested in stocks or a stake that is all of her liquid wealth, and not in the two-sided way these richer benchmarks would require.

None of the first four caveats enters the computation of Benchmark S, which requires only return moments and preferences. Reassuringly, the one existing full life-cycle treatment of LLM advice reaches the same corner as Benchmark I: Choukhmane et al. (2026) find that a calibrated Cocco et al. (2005) model implies near-universal equity holding for young and middle-aged average earners, and that it cannot rationalize the more conservative shares LLMs actually recommend early in life.
