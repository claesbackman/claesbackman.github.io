3Experimental Design

The experiment has two arms. The main arm poses three financial questions to thirteen frontier LLMs in twenty-one countries and varies the language of each question, the country it refers to, and the local financial context it provides. The stated-investor arm varies the investor rather than the country, by stating her risk tolerance, pension generosity, or investment horizon in an otherwise fixed English prompt. I first describe the three questions and the country selection, then the prompt versions and the contrasts they identify, the translation workflow, the stated-investor arm, the models and API parameters, and the data collection. Appendix Table A1 lists the prompt language, the local-currency investment amount, and the full Version C vocabulary for every country, and Appendix E documents the technical implementation of the data collection.

3.1The Three Questions

Each LLM answers three financial question: a primary portfolio-allocation question and two secondary questions on mortgage prepayment and inflation protection. Question 1 asks how to split the local-currency equivalent of $50,000 between a broad stock-market index fund and government bonds over a five-year horizon. The outcome is the fraction allocated to equities.

Question 2 asks whether to invest savings in a stock-market index fund or to use them to pay down an existing 4 percent mortgage with twenty years remaining. The outcome is the fraction allocated to the index fund. This question has a sharper normative benchmark than Question 1, because mortgage prepayment is a risk-free investment whose return is pinned down by the contract rate. I use a a pre-specified rule that assigns any country whose prevailing mortgage rate exceeds twice the prompted 4 percent to a premise-acceptance group. In these countries the question also tests whether the models flag the 4 percent rate as unusual for the local environment or simply condition on it. In the end, all models conditioned on it.

Question 3 asks how to protect the real value of $50,000 sitting in a savings account that earns 1 percent nominal while annual inflation is 3 percent. The outcome has two parts: a numeric fraction to invest and a categorical primary instrument (equities, inflation-linked bonds, real estate, gold, or other). Because the stated 3 percent inflation rate is far below prevailing inflation in some countries, Question 3 was ex ante the question most likely to lead the models to reject the premise and withhold a numerical answer. A pre-specified contingency therefore promotes the refusal indicator to the primary Question 3 outcome if the refusal rate exceeds 10 percent in any country–model cell. The refusal rate was zero across 37,021 model calls in 273 country \(\times\) model cells, so the contingency never applied. Several models noted that the inflation rate was unrealistically low for the country, most often for Turkey, but none refused to answer.

3.2Country Selection

I chose the country set to span meaningful cross-country variation in pension generosity, equity-market development, inflation history, and measured preferences, while keeping translation risk low. I imposed one screening constraint. The preference regressions of Section 5.2 require each country’s measured risk preferences, so the Global Preferences Survey (Falk et al. 2018) must cover every country. This requirement excludes otherwise natural candidates such as Denmark.

The United States serves as the baseline for the country contrasts. I prompt five further countries in English (Australia, Canada, India, South Africa, and the United Kingdom). They add observations at essentially no translation risk while spanning very different pension institutions, from compulsory superannuation to a replacement rate among the lowest in the panel.

The fifteen non-English countries include high-literacy European systems with structurally different occupational pensions (Sweden, Germany, the Netherlands, Switzerland) and further European variation (France, Spain, Italy, Poland, Finland). Outside Europe they cover the Asian markets (China, Japan, South Korea), several high-inflation economies (Brazil, Turkey), and Mexico.

3.3Prompt Structure and Version Design

Each prompt has two blocks. An Instructions block, written in English in every cell, asks for a JSON response that contains the recommendation and a word-limited explanation. A Question block describes the investor and poses the financial question, and the treatments change only this block. Keeping the instructions in English holds response-format compliance constant across languages. Section 4.6 tests this choice directly by re-collecting the primary cell with the instructions translated as well.

The Question block varies along three dimensions. The first is the country and its prompt language. I prompt six countries in English (the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, India, and South Africa) and the fifteen others in their local language, with Switzerland in German and Mexico in Spanish (Appendix Table A1 lists each pairing). The second is the investor’s stated age (30, 40, or 55), which varies for the primary equity-share question and is fixed at 40 for the two secondary questions.

The third dimension is the prompt version. I use three versions of the prompt, described in detail below: the first version uses the local language with little or no local context, the second version uses English but states the investor location, and the third includes local-context in the form of pension and fund names. I use these different prompts to decompose the country effect into language, country-naming, and local-framing components.3 The version lettering follows the pre-registration. Version A denoted a translated prompt that named no country. I dropped it before collection because it could not separate the effect of the question’s language from the style of any particular translation, and because the comparison of Versions B and B-cross identifies the language effect more cleanly by holding the country reference fixed. I keep the labels of the surviving versions unchanged so that the paper matches the registered design.

More specifically, Version B states “I live in [country]” but uses a generic instrument vocabulary (“a broad stock market index fund,” “government bonds,” “a standard pension plan”). In the fifteen non-English countries it appears in the local language. Its investment amount is the round local-currency equivalent of $50,000 (e.g. 500,000 kronor in Sweden, 45,000 euros in Germany, 40,000 pounds in the United Kingdom), written in the native local number format (“500 000 kr,” “45.000 Euro”). Version B-cross retains the country reference (“I live in [country]”) and the local-currency investment amount of Version B but reverts the question to English. It is collected only for the fifteen non-English countries. Note that the baseline prompts does not adjust for PPP or income. In a robustness check I also adjust the stake by PPP, which does not alter the result.

Version C keeps the language, country reference, and investment amount of Version B but replaces the generic financial vocabulary with country-specific equivalents: local index funds (e.g. an index fund via Avanza or Nordnet in Sweden, eMAXIS Slim All-Country Equity in Japan), local government bonds (Svenska statsobligationer, JGBs, Tesouro Direto), and local pension references (tjänstepension, bAV, PER, iDeCo).

Three contrasts identify the components of the country effect. The cross-country comparison of Version B identifies the joint country-and-language effect relative to the U.S. baseline. The within-country comparison of Versions B-cross and B holds the country reference fixed and changes only the language of the question, which identifies the language effect. The within-country comparison of Versions C and B holds both country and language fixed and changes only whether the prompt names local instruments and pensions, which identifies the local-framing effect.

3.4Translation Workflow

I produced the Version B and Version C question blocks for the fifteen non-English countries through an automated pipeline that translates only the Question block and keeps the protected financial terms and currency amounts verbatim. Two automated checks screened the machine output, a term-protection check and a full back-translation. I then had native-speaker review each version and provide feedback in all fifteen translated countries. In the English-prompting countries, a two-vendor LLM cross-check verified the local-instrument wording of Version C. Before I filed the pre-registration, every assembled prompt passed a consistency audit with zero failures, an adversarial LLM-based review covered all ninety unique translated blocks, and I read every block in full. I then froze the translations for collection. Appendix E.2 documents the pipeline mechanics, the two automated checks, and the review-and-correction protocol.

3.5The Stated-Investor Arm

The second arm varies the investor herself instead of the country or language, but repeats the exercise for every country. The stated-investor arm takes the English Question 1 prompt at age 40 and inserts statements about the investor. The resulting prompts are otherwise identical to Version B in the six English-prompting countries and to Version B-cross in the fifteen others, with the same local-currency amounts. I began with three pre-registered checks, and added three more three exploratory probes afterwards. I now describe these probes.

The main pre-registered arm has six treatment cells, related to risk-tolerance, pension generosity and the investment horizon. The two risk-tolerance statements and the two pension statements are crossed, which gives four cells, and two horizon cells stand alone. The risk-tolerance statements are length-matched qualitative sentences: “I am comfortable with substantial fluctuations in my portfolio value, including potential short-term losses, if it means higher expected returns over time” versus “I am uncomfortable with large fluctuations in my portfolio value and would prefer to limit potential losses, even if it means lower expected returns.” The pension statements are “I will receive a substantial pension when I retire, projected to replace most of my pre-retirement income” versus “I will receive only a small pension when I retire, covering a modest fraction of my pre-retirement income.” The horizon cells replace the phrase “over a five-year horizon” with a one-year or a twenty-year horizon and insert no other statement. I pre-registered three hypotheses as secondary confirmatory tests. Each is a single pre-specified coefficient, reported without multiplicity correction and with the same wild-cluster-bootstrap inference as the primary regressions.

After examining the pre-registered results, I also added three exploratory probes to the arm. Each is a single English sentence in the same statement slot that states one part of the investor’s balance sheet. Specifically, I add a a pension-composition probe, which states either “My pension is a guaranteed, inflation-linked annuity that will pay a fixed real income in retirement regardless of how financial markets perform” or “My pension is invested almost entirely in stocks, so its eventual value will rise and fall with equity-market performance.” I also add a hedging probe, which states either “The stock fund I am considering is currency-hedged to my home currency, so its returns are not exposed to exchange-rate movements” or “The stock fund I am considering is a global fund with no currency hedging, so its returns also move with exchange rates.” Finally, I also added an arm that varies how much of the investors’ wealth the stake represents, moving from all, about half, or about a third of the investor’s liquid wealth. I first collected all seven probe cells for the United States, at fifty repetitions per model and cell (4,550 queries), and then extended the composition and hedging probes to the other twenty countries at twenty-five repetitions (26,000 queries). Both collections ran in August 2026. The estimates use the no-statement Version B and B-cross cells of the July collection as the reference.

3.6Models, Repetitions, and API Parameters

The experiment queries thirteen frontier LLMs from six vendors through the OpenRouter unified API. Four vendors are U.S.-headquartered: Anthropic (Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Opus 4.8), OpenAI (GPT-5-mini, GPT-5.5), Google (Gemini 3 Flash, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Gemini 3.1 Pro), and xAI (Grok 4.3). So that the panel does not consist solely of U.S.-headquartered firms, I add DeepSeek (DeepSeek V4 Flash, DeepSeek V4 Pro), a non-U.S. vendor whose models are primarily trained on Chinese-language data, and Mistral (Mistral Medium 3.5), a European (French) vendor.

A cell is one combination of country, version, question, age, and model. I query each cell fifty times at temperature 0.7, with no system prompt and a maximum response length of 4,000 tokens. Note that the unit of inference is the model, which means that fifty repetitions suffice. Additional repetitions would reduce only the within-cell sampling term, which is already small relative to between-model heterogeneity.

In total the design comprises 267,150 queries, of which 185,250 form the country/language arm and 81,900 the stated-investor arm. Appendix Table A2 gives the accounting by arm, version, and question. The post-filing balance-sheet probes of Section 6.1 add a further 30,550 queries, which I collected into a separate corpus.

3.7Data Collection and Parsing

Collection proceeded in two stages, an exploratory pilot followed by the pre-registered main collection. A staged pilot of roughly 6,800 queries, completed in late June 2026, exercised the full pipeline end to end.4 I made filing the pre-registration conditional on the pilot passing three pre-specified success criteria: a JSON parse rate of at least 95 percent per model, refusal rates below pre-specified ceilings in every country–model cell, and a between-model dispersion of the country contrast no larger than 0.06. The pilot passed all three, with a parse rate near 99.6 percent and no refusals, and it produced no truncated responses. I filed a pre-registration at the Open Science Framework (OSF) on July 2, 2026, with the prompts frozen. The main collection submitted all 267,150 queries through the OpenRouter API on July 3–4, 2026. The two pre-registered collection-robustness cells—temperature bookends at 0 and 1.0 and a Version B variant that states the investment amount in U.S. dollars—added 8,450 queries on July 6, 2026.

After the main collection, I added five exploratory collections, none of which is part of the pre-registration: the three balance-sheet probes of the stated-investor arm (August 2026, Section 6.1), a model-tracking wave with a panel of twenty-one models (September 2026), and three re-collections of the primary cell in October 2026 that translate the Instructions block, let the model ask questions before advising, and restate the stake at purchasing-power parity (Section 4.6).

The main run covered the full design grid, with every cell reaching its fifty repetitions and no duplicates or unresolved API errors. The parser recovered a numeric recommendation from 99.9 percent of responses, the collection produced no refusals, and no model changed versions during the run. I exclude the unparsed responses and, under the pre-registered truncation rule, those that exhausted the 4,000-token budget. Appendix E.4 documents the parser, the refusal rule, and the model-identity checks.
