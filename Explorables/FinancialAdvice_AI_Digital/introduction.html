1Introduction

Retail investors are increasingly turning to artificial intelligence (AI) for financial advice, through chatbots built on large language models (LLMs) (OECD 2026). The share of U.S. adults who use AI tools specifically for financial advice roughly doubled between 2024 and 2025 (Financial Health Network 2025), and over half of U.K. adults now use such tools to help manage their money (Lloyds Banking Group 2025). This represents a fundamental shift in the nature of financial advice for households all around the world, from national markets in which advice reflects local norms and institutions to a global market in which it comes from a handful of foundation models trained overwhelmingly on U.S.-English text. This shift raises several questions about the new global financial advisor: What financial advice do AI models give in different countries and do they tailor the advice to local circumstances?

To answer these questions, I use a pre-registered experiment in which the subjects are thirteen frontier AI models from six vendors and the tasks are three financial problems posed in twenty-one countries. My first finding on the new supply side of financial advice is that AI financial advice is largely the same everywhere for a simple portfolio choice question, but that questions requiring more local context generate more dispersion. Asked how to split the local-currency equivalent of $50,000 between a broad stock-market index fund and government bonds, the models give advice that differs only modestly across countries. Compared to the United States, every significant contrast is negative, ranging from 2.7 percentage points less equity in Mexico to 11 points less in Brazil. What instead moves the advice is the identity of the model, which accounts for 69.5 percent of the variance against 5.7 percent for country. Asked whether to invest savings or use them to prepay a 4 percent mortgage, the models recommend investing 10 to 29 percentage points less than in the United States in each of the fifteen countries where that rate is realistic. The largest gaps are in Japan, China, Switzerland, and Germany. Asked how to protect savings from 3 percent inflation, the models switch from equities to inflation-linked bonds in Brazil, Turkey, and Mexico, but overwhelmingly recommends equities in all other countries. On these two questions model identity explains only 15 and 26 percent of the variance. The pattern suggests that the models localize when the question depends on the local economy.

The cross-country differences in advice raises the question of what, if anything, the advice should track. Giving the same advice everywhere on the portfolio is not a mistake per se: under integrated markets the risky portfolio is the same world index everywhere. Of course, there are actually some differences across countries in the advice, so the models are not just giving exactly the same advice everywhere. As a simple (but not realistic) benchmark, a simple Merton benchmark with a currency-hedged world fund prescribes 0.52 in every country, within about six points of the advice everywhere. However, this model cannot explain why some countries receive less equity than the United States.1 Another variable that differ across countries and could affect portfolio choice is currency risk (Campbell and Viceira 2002). While it is feasible that the models could take this into account, an updated benchmark with unhedged local-currency moments prescribes equity shares from 0.21 in Turkey to 0.53 in South Korea, yet mean advice spans only 0.46 to 0.58. Later on, I also show that providing the models with information about currency hedging has a neglible impact on advice. A more realistic benchmark would take local pension systems and background wealth into account, but as I show later, the models do not measurably adapt their advice based on these variables. An alternative approach to understanding what models track across countries is to relate cross-country differences to country-level fundamentals. However, these also seem to have limited explanatory power: country differences do not track measured risk preferences, financial literacy, local market returns or the prescriptions of a standard portfolio benchmark. I relate the estimated country fixed effects to country-level risk-taking from the Global Preferences Survey (Falk et al. 2018) and to financial literacy from the S&P Global FinLit Survey (Klapper et al. 2015), but find only a weak relationship with the average equity share a country receives.

Instead, the third finding is that AI models give the kind of “popular financial advice” studied by Canner et al. (1997) and Choi (2022). The evidence from this comes from several different tests. First, I insert statements about the investor into otherwise identical English prompts for each country, covering risk tolerance, currency hedging, pension composition, pension generosity, the share of her liquid wealth that the stake represents, and investment horizon. Stated risk tolerance raises the recommended equity share by 46 percentage points, consistent with the dominance of risk tolerance in Ross and Lo (2026). A twenty-year rather than one-year horizon raises the equity share by 40 points, in contrast to the classic Merton–Samuelson irrelevance result where horizon should not affect the share at all under CRRA preferences with i.i.d. returns. Second, I ask the models to list the questions they would put to the investor before advising. In nearly every country, the model asks the same four things in the same order: risk tolerance, what the money is for, whether the five-year horizon is firm, and whether an emergency fund exists. Risk tolerance comes first in 84 percent of responses. These questions are reminiscent of popular financial advice, and it is striking that there is only neglible differences across countries. The models also neglect to ask about the household balance sheet, which would be highly relevant in e.g. life-cycle models.

I also conduct several checks that directly asks how the balance sheet affects the advice. A stated generous pension adds safe background wealth, so it should raise the equity share if the model integrates the investor’s balance sheet. However, the advice barrely moves when information about pensions is added to the prompt. This is not because the models ignore pensions. Nearly every explanation mentions the investor’s existing pension. In additional tests, added after the pre-registered results were examined, I find that the advice moves strongly and in the predicted direction when a stated balance-sheet cue raises the investor’s exposure, as with a pension invested in stocks or a stake equal to her entire liquid wealth, but it barely moves on cues that signal safety, such as a guaranteed annuity, a currency hedge, or a cash cushion. The models appear to read the balance sheet as a source of hazard rather than as the two-sided input of portfolio theory. Overall, the advice fits a retail risk questionnaire, rather than advice that tracks the household’s balance sheet.

The fourth and final finding is that the models appear to solve the portfolio problem in a shared, English-anchored representation regardless of the prompt’s language, and then explain the answer in the local language and with local context. This is consistent with the interpretability literature, which finds that multilingual models solve tasks in a shared, English-leaning concept space (Wendler et al. 2024; Tang et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2025; Lindsey et al. 2025). One response in Swedish, a language I speak, describes a “glidväg” (‘glide path’), a direct translation of the English industry term with no particular meaning in Swedish.2 The formal term in Swedish is generationsfond or a reference to the default public AP7 fund. These terms do not appear often in the explanations, however: generationsfond appears zero times across roughly 5 million lines of response text, while the calque glidväg appears in 0.3 percent of Swedish responses. More formally, I machine-code the explanation that each model gives for its recommendation into four binary indicators. Local financial context appears in 18 percent of U.S. explanations but 29 to 78 percentage points more often in every other country. Naming local instruments in the prompt raises local references by 23 percentage points while moving the allocation itself by less than a point. The explanations thus localize far more than the advice. The translation changes the words of the answer but not the advice.

Summing up, this paper characterizes the supply side of a newly integrated market for AI financial advice. Financial advice was a nationally segmented service industry defined by national regulation, by relationships, and by language (for an overview, see Reuter and Schoar 2024). AI models make this into a global market, where the advice is relatively similar and appears anchored on retail risk questionnaires. Instead of a national advice market, AI financial advice derives from a handful of foundation models. This is not necessarily a negative for the quality of financial advice, and it may well be that AI models are better advisors than humans. Human advisors already gave one-size-fits-all advice, with advisor fixed effects dominating client characteristics (Foerster et al. 2017). However, we may still want to know how much of the geographic differentiation in advice reflects economically relevant differences, and how much reflects local convention? What should the advice be for investors in different countries and under different circumstances?

Answering these questions is not trivial, and require us to take a normative stance of what the advice should be across countries. Although this is not the purpose of this study, standard finance theory and the results speak toward this question in part. First, the models differentiate across countries on decisions set by local prices and barely on the risky share. For the risky share, the same risky is possible to motivate with a (currency-hedged) world fund. The models are relatively close to this benchmark, with advice compressed toward a common level at or a few points below the U.S. baseline. The differences are present in some countries, but do not track fundamentals well. For the mortgage question, for example, the local real rate decides the answer, and the models produce larger differences. However, it is not clear why the country constrasts are negative for all countries. Second, the advice follows a retail risk questionnaire rather than the household’s balance sheet. This is potentially more problematic in countries where local circumstances differ more from the US, and where the advisor should be asking about local circumstances. Even if a human was to provide information about their pension, this still does not move the advice. Third, the models provide local explanations for their advice, even if the local explanations do not change the advice. For an investor in a country outside the US, it is difficult to see that the models are giving them the same advice as investors in the US because the explanation will localize.
