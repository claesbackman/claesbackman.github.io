2Literature

There is a burgeoning literature on AI financial advice, which also relates closely to an older literature on robo-advising and traditional financial advice. Beginning with this newer literature, Choukhmane et al. (2026) elicit prompts from a representative U.S. sample, simulate the life-cycle paths that following the advice would generate, and confront those paths with the prescriptions of a calibrated life-cycle model. They find that current models give moderately risk-averse advice that is broadly consistent with life-cycle theory, yet relies on round-number heuristics and varies with the gender and financial literacy of the person asking. Ross and Lo (2026) instead recover the advice rule from the mapping between inputs and outputs. They draw a thousand synthetic client profiles with near-orthogonal characteristics, collect allocations from a single vendor’s model family, and fit interpretable surrogate models. They find what they call heuristic collapse: self-reported risk tolerance carries most of the predictive weight, while age, income, horizon, and liquidity contribute marginally.

This paper departs from these papers on three margins. First, both papers hold the investor’s country fixed, so neither can ask whether the advice is calibrated to the institutions, asset markets, and preferences of the country in which the household lives. Second, Choukhmane et al. (2026) record which topics the advice text covers, whereas I code the explanation across countries to ask whether the reasons the model gives track local fundamentals. Third, I test a channel of risk-taking capacity, which is different from the willingness to take risk, by stating the generosity of the investor’s pension in the prompt.

Beyond these two papers, this paper contributes to several different literatures. To the economics of robo-advising and algorithmic financial advice, I add evidence on whether the newest generation of algorithmic advisors localizes its recommendations. Earlier robo-advisors improve diversification and reduce behavioral biases (D’Acunto et al. 2019), deliver substantial welfare gains (Rossi and Utkus 2024), and more broadly move household behavior closer to normative prescriptions (D’Acunto and Rossi 2023). LLMs differ from these robo-advisors in two ways. They are general-purpose rather than domain-restricted,, and they reach essentially every language and country at once. These models therefore loosens the supply-side constraints that, together with demand, determine who accesses and benefits from advice (Reuter and Schoar 2024). Any country-specific bias in their training data then propagates globally.

To the literature on the quality of retail financial advice, this paper adds evidence on what kind of advisor AI is. Canner et al. (1997) show that popular advisors recommend a higher ratio of bonds to stocks for more conservative investors, contrary to the mutual-fund separation theorem, and Choi (2022) documents how popular personal financial advice departs from the prescriptions of economic theory. Human advisors also give largely one-size-fits-all advice, with advisor fixed effects explaining more of the variation in client portfolios than client characteristics do (Foerster et al. 2017). AI models reproduce both patterns across the twenty-one countries I study. Model identity explains most of the variance in the recommended equity share and the country explains little, much as advisor identity outweighs client characteristics in human advice.

To the literature on cross-country differences in household financial behavior, this paper adds a new margin: the advice households receive from AI models, as distinct from the choices they make. Equity-market participation varies substantially across countries, and income, wealth, and institutional access do not fully explain the variation (Guiso and Sodini 2013; Christelis et al. 2013). The Global Preferences Survey (Falk et al. 2018) provides validated country-level measures of preferences that correlate with savings and investment behavior, and financial literacy strongly predicts equity-market participation (Rooij et al. 2011). Advice tailored to each country’s clientele would vary with these measures. I find only a weak relationship between either measure and the equity share that AI models recommend in a country.

To the literature on demographic bias in machine-learning models and LLMs (Bolukbasi et al. 2016; Navigli et al. 2023), this paper adds both a new treatment and a decomposition. In credit markets, machine-learning scoring changes the distribution of mortgage approvals across racial groups (Fuster et al. 2022). In LLM advice, Foltyn and Olsson (2026) document gender gaps on a stock-bond allocation question that this paper adapts as its primary outcome. I keep the structure of their question and replace demographic identity with country and language as the treatment. This dimension has first-order policy relevance because AI advisory tools reach investors across borders while pension and tax systems remain national.

To the literature on how multilingual language models process and retrieve knowledge, this paper adds a behavioral test on an economic task. Interpretability studies find that the middle layers of multilingual models operate in a shared concept space that lies closer to English than to any other language (Wendler et al. 2024; Tang et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2025; Lindsey et al. 2025). Knowledge-probing studies instead find that the same model often asserts different facts depending on the query language (Kassner et al. 2021; Qi et al. 2023). These two findings make different predictions for a translated financial question. Shared processing predicts nearly the same advice in every language, whereas language-keyed retrieval predicts advice that inherits the conventions of each language’s financial text. Asking in the local language rather than in English lowers the recommended equity share by only 1.3 percentage points, which supports shared processing, and the local detail the models add appears in the explanation rather than in the allocation. The questions that the models would like to ask investors also varies very little across countries, which again supports shared processing.
