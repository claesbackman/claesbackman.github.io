5When Should the Advice Differ Across Countries?

The experimental results establish whether LLM advice varies across countries, but not whether that variation, or its absence, is appropriate. Two properties separate warranted from unwarranted geographic differentiation. The first is invariance. Holding the investor’s preferences, investment opportunities, and balance sheet fixed, the advice should not change merely because the investor names a different country or asks in a different language. The second is responsiveness. Holding the country fixed, the advice should move when a determinant of the optimal allocation changes, in the direction and roughly by the amount that theory prescribes. A lower recommended equity share in Brazil than in the United States therefore violates invariance if the two investors face the same economics, and reflects responsiveness if Brazil’s fundamentals warrant more conservative advice. This section states a benchmark that separates the two (Section 5.1), asks whether the country effects track measured national preferences and literacy (Section 5.2), and evaluates the mortgage advice against a benchmark pinned by the contract rate (Section 5.3). On the risky share the benchmark warrants little geographic differentiation unless the world fund is held unhedged, and the advice shows little. On the mortgage the benchmark warrants large differences, and the advice produces them in the right direction, but compressed and tilted toward investing. Appendix C develops the benchmarks in full.

5.1Which country differences the benchmark warrants

The simplest benchmark takes the prompt at face value and treats the $50,000 stake as a standalone portfolio of a world equity index and domestic government bonds (Benchmark S). For an investor with constant relative risk aversion \(\gamma\), the optimal equity share is the rule of Merton (1969) and Samuelson (1969), \[\theta^{*}_{c} \;=\; \frac{\mu_{c}-r_{c}}{\gamma\,\sigma_{c}^{2}}, \tag{5}\] where \(\mu_{c}-r_{c}\) is the expected excess return and \(\sigma_{c}\) the volatility of the equity fund, both measured in local currency. Appendix C gives the calibration. The share depends neither on the size of the stake nor, with independent returns, on the horizon, so neither the amount nor the five-year horizon in the prompt enters the benchmark. Because the equity premium is common across currencies by uncovered interest parity, the benchmark varies across countries only through \(\sigma_{c}\) at a common \(\gamma\).

How much the benchmark varies depends on whether the world fund is hedged. A hedged fund carries roughly the U.S.-dollar volatility of the index in every currency, so the prescribed share is 0.52 everywhere, and mean advice lies within about six percentage points of it in every country. An unhedged fund adds exchange-rate risk to its local-currency volatility, and at \(\gamma=3\) the prescribed share then ranges from 0.21 in Turkey and 0.23 in Brazil to 0.53 in South Korea, a spread of thirty-two percentage points against the twelve the advice spans. The advice follows little of that spread. Regressing the country effects on the benchmark’s prescribed deviations from the United States gives a calibration slope of \(\hat{\kappa}=0.13\), so the advice transmits about an eighth of the cross-country variation that the unhedged benchmark prescribes. The slope measures responsiveness to the one fundamental that varies across countries in the standalone benchmark. A slope of one means full pass-through of the currency risk the unhedged benchmark prices, and a slope of zero means none. The wild bootstrap over countries rejects full calibration (\(p<0.001\) against \(\kappa=1\)) and does not reject the U.S.-default hypothesis H2 (\(p=0.32\) against \(\kappa=0\)). Dropping Brazil lowers the slope to 0.03, dropping Turkey raises it to 0.21, and dropping any other country leaves it between 0.11 and 0.15, so the estimate depends on the two countries with the most volatile currencies but never comes near full calibration. Following the advice rather than the unhedged benchmark costs an investor in the median country only 1.6 basis points of the stake per year, about $40 over the prompt’s five-year horizon, but costs an investor in Turkey 137 basis points per year, about $3,500. Appendix C reports the country gaps under both hedging assumptions, the sensitivity of \(\hat{\kappa}\) to risk aversion and to preferences calibrated on measured risk-taking, the influence diagnostics, and the welfare cost of the gaps.

Adding the rest of the balance sheet does not restore a cross-country spread. Treating pension wealth and human capital as safe outside wealth \(B_{c}\) beside the stake \(W\) (Benchmark I) scales the equity demand of the liquid portfolio by total wealth over liquid wealth, so that both benchmarks take the form \[\alpha^{*}_{c} \;=\; \min\left\{1,\; \frac{\mu_{c}-r_{c}}{\gamma\,\sigma_{c}^{2}}\left(1+\frac{B_{c}}{W}\right)\right\}, \tag{6}\] with \(B_{c}=0\) for Benchmark S. Because outside wealth is an order of magnitude larger than the stake, the prescribed share reaches the 100 percent corner in every country, far above any recommendation in the data (Appendix C.3). Only the unhedged standalone benchmark therefore prescribes different advice across countries, and the verdict on the advice turns on whether an advisor should penalize unhedged currency risk. If it should, the compression documented in Section 4 is a failure of responsiveness. If it should not, the near-uniform level of the advice satisfies invariance, and the open question becomes the country effects that remain, such as Brazil’s eleven-point gap from the United States, which have no counterpart in a benchmark that prescribes 0.52 everywhere.

Equation (6) makes the two properties concrete and organizes the remaining tests of the paper. Anything outside the equation should leave the advice unchanged once the economics is fixed: the country name, the language of the question, the local names of the instruments, and, with independent returns, the investment horizon. The terms inside the equation should move it. The equity premium \(\mu_{c}-r_{c}\) is common across currencies by uncovered interest parity. The volatility \(\sigma_{c}\) differs across countries only for an unhedged fund, the margin this subsection prices. Risk aversion \(\gamma\) belongs to the investor. The Version B prompt does not state it, so the benchmark holds it common, and the stated-tolerance cells of Section 6 move it directly. Outside wealth \(B_{c}\) varies across countries with pension generosity and the wage profile, and the stated-pension cells of Section 6 move it for a given investor. Appendix C.3 extends the equation to outside wealth that carries equity risk. One caveat limits the invariance tests. The primary prompt leaves the hedging of the fund, taxes, the real return on local bonds, and the rest of the balance sheet to the model, so a country effect can reflect a fundamental that the model infers from the country name. The design identifies the effect of each prompt treatment, but not whether a country-name effect is justified by fundamentals the benchmark omits. Table 2 in Section 6.2 collects the evidence on both properties.

5.2Does the advice track measured preferences or literacy?

This subsection asks whether the country effects track two country-level measures that a culturally calibrated advisor might respond to, the willingness to take risks in the Global Preferences Survey (Falk et al. 2018) and the share of adults classified as financially literate in the S&P Global FinLit Survey (Klapper et al. 2015). Advice tailored to a country’s clientele would recommend more equity where measured risk-taking is higher and, because literacy predicts equity-market participation at the household level (Rooij et al. 2011), where more adults are financially literate. I test the two predictions, H7 and H8, with pre-registered second-stage regressions of the country fixed effects of equation (1), written \(\hat{b}_{c}\) here to avoid a clash with risk aversion \(\gamma\), on each measure. These slopes differ from the calibration slope \(\kappa\) of Section 5.1, which regresses the same country effects on the benchmark’s prescribed deviations. Invariance does not require the advice to track either measure, because the prompt describes a single investor rather than a country mean, and imputing a country’s average risk-taking to that individual is an ecological inference the advisor need not make. The tests are therefore descriptive. They ask whether the models impute national averages, not whether they should.9 Each test is a bivariate slope over the twenty non-U.S. countries, with the same inference as the calibration slope: a wild bootstrap over countries (Webb weights, 9,999 replications) as the primary procedure, standard errors alongside, and a two-stage bootstrap that propagates the first-stage uncertainty in \(\hat{b}_{c}\). Like the calibration slope \(\kappa\), both hypotheses sit at the second altitude of the registration. They are pre-specified in full and registered as descriptive, and a dated amendment froze the covariate vectors before I estimated any second-stage coefficient. Because that amendment post-dates the pilot, I keep these tests descriptive rather than confirmatory.

Figure 6 plots both relationships. Panel A shows that the advice does not track measured risk preferences. The H7 slope is \(0.028\) (standard error \(0.021\)), and the wild bootstrap over countries does not reject zero (\(p = 0.272\), 95 percent confidence interval \([-0.058, 0.203]\)). The point estimate is not only imprecise but economically small. A country one full standard deviation more risk-tolerant than another receives under three percentage points more equity, and even the widest preference contrast in the panel, from Japan at \(-0.36\) to South Africa at \(0.97\), predicts a gap of under four points. The country effects at these two endpoints show the same absence of a gradient. Japan receives advice statistically indistinguishable from the U.S. baseline (Section 4.2), while South Africa, by far the most risk-tolerant country, receives slightly less equity than the baseline rather than more.

Panel B tells the same story for literacy. The H8 slope is \(0.055\) (standard error \(0.040\), wild bootstrap \(p = 0.178\), confidence interval \([-0.030, 0.151]\)), and moving across the full literacy range in the panel, from 24 percent of adults in India and Turkey to 71 percent in Sweden, changes predicted advice by under three percentage points. Even this small positive slope owes much to which countries sit at each end of the literacy distribution. The high-income English-prompting countries (the United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia) and the Nordics (Sweden and Finland) all rank among the seven most literate countries in the panel, and their advice sits at the baseline level. The bottom of the distribution mixes countries with large negative effects (Brazil, Italy) and countries with small or no effects (Turkey, South Korea).10 The pre-registered diagnostics do not change this reading. Weighting countries by the precision of their first-stage estimates moves the slopes to \(0.022\) and \(0.052\). Neither estimate is the artifact of a single country: leave-one-out slopes range from \(0.015\) (dropping Brazil) to \(0.043\) (dropping Japan) for risk-taking and from \(0.034\) to \(0.073\) for literacy, always positive and always small. The two-stage bootstrap, which resamples the thirteen models and re-estimates the country effects on every draw, produces intervals of \([0.016, 0.040]\) for H7 and \([0.013, 0.099]\) for H8, both excluding zero. The country effects are therefore precise enough that the positive slopes are not first-stage noise, but the twenty countries scatter too widely around so flat a line for the association to qualify as a cross-country regularity.

Read against the two properties, the three slopes mean different things. The flat slopes on preferences and literacy are consistent with invariance, since the models do not impute national averages to the individual investor. The flat calibration slope is a failure of responsiveness if an advisor should price unhedged currency risk. Whatever drives the country effects, it is neither the currency risk that the unhedged benchmark prices nor the measured preferences or literacy of the populations being advised. Every effect that survives the Holm correction is in a country prompted in a language other than English (Section 4.2). That grouping bundles the language of the question with everything else the English-prompting countries share with the U.S. baseline, and the within-country comparison of Section 4.3 attributes only about a point to language itself.

5.3A benchmark for the mortgage question

Section 4.5 shows that the mortgage advice varies across countries far more than the portfolio advice. The mortgage question also admits a sharper benchmark against which to judge that variation, because prepaying a mortgage is a risk-free investment whose return is pinned by the contract rate. The relevant safe return is the prompted 4 percent net of the deductible share of interest and of expected inflation. Prepayment is therefore a better real investment where inflation is low, as in Japan, and a worse one where mortgage interest is deductible. Replacing \(r_{c}\) in the Merton rule (5) with this after-tax real mortgage rate gives the benchmark share \(\alpha^{Q2}_{c}\) (Appendix C.8). Because effective deductibility depends on income and itemization, the appendix also reports \(\alpha^{Q2}_{c}\) across a band of deductible shares from zero to one half. The benchmark for the five premise-acceptance countries of Section 3 takes the prompt’s counterfactual 4 percent rate at face value, so I report them separately from the other sixteen, the calibration countries. The pre-registration does not specify this benchmark, so the comparison is exploratory.

Figure 7 plots the mortgage advice against this benchmark, and Appendix Table A21 reports the country values. The advice tilts toward investing rather than prepaying in every calibration country. The recommended index-fund share exceeds \(\alpha^{Q2}_{c}\) in all sixteen, by thirteen to thirty-six percentage points and by twenty-eight on average. The tilt is not an artifact of the deductibility assumption, since in fourteen of the sixteen the advice exceeds even the equity-favorable end of the deductibility band. Its size is nearly constant where the benchmark lies below 0.40 and shrinks above it. In the nine non-U.S. calibration countries whose benchmark lies below 0.40, from Japan, where the after-tax real mortgage rate is highest, to Sweden, the gap stays between 29 and 34 points, so the advice rises with the benchmark almost point for point. In the six non-U.S. calibration countries with the highest benchmarks, Spain, the Netherlands, Italy, Australia, China, and South Korea, the advice stops rising and the gap narrows to between 13 and 22 points. The United States is the exception. Its benchmark of 0.47 is among the highest, yet its advice of 0.83 is the highest in the panel and its gap of 36 points the largest of the sixteen.

In the five premise-acceptance countries, whose benchmark takes the prompt’s 4 percent rate at face value, average advice sits within four points of the U.S. level. Two of them, South Africa and India, have the highest benchmarks in the panel, 0.98 and 0.79. The prompted rate lies below local inflation in both, and their currency volatility is moderate, so the benchmark prescribes near-full investment, while the advice stays at about 0.80. Advice therefore falls short of the benchmark only in South Africa and matches it in India. The group average conceals disagreement across models. Mistral Medium 3.5 and DeepSeek V4 Flash recommend 8 to 16 points less investment than at their own U.S. level in all five premise-acceptance countries, consistent with replacing the prompted rate with the higher local one, while Gemini 3.5 Flash recommends 12 to 17 points more in Brazil, Mexico, and Turkey.

On the fifteen non-U.S. calibration countries, the calibration regression of Section 5.1 gives \(\hat{\kappa}^{Q2} = 0.24\) for the mortgage question, against \(0.07\) for the allocation question on the same fifteen countries. The mortgage advice thus transmits about a quarter of the cross-country variation its benchmark prescribes, roughly three times the share for the allocation advice. With fifteen countries neither slope is statistically distinguishable from zero, and the 95 percent confidence intervals of both exclude full calibration at one. The compression that flattens the portfolio advice therefore reappears on the mortgage question, especially among the high-benchmark countries, where the advice stops rising. On the decision where the benchmark warrants the largest geographic differences, the models are responsive in direction but not in magnitude.
