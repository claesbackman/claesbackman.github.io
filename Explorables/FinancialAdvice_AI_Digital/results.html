4Main Results

This section reports how LLM advice varies across countries. I first document the raw patterns in the advice, then I estimate the country effects on the primary question, decompose them into language and local framing, and ask whether they track measured risk preferences and financial literacy. I then turn to the mortgage and inflation questions and close with robustness. The pre-registered test of age and pension generosity is reported with the other tests of the advice rule in Section 6.2.

Except for the two secondary questions in Section 4.4, the outcome is the recommended equity fraction on Question 1. Every regression includes model fixed effects, so each contrast compares the advice of a single model. I cluster standard errors at the model level.5 Because thirteen clusters are too few for reliable asymptotic inference, every reported \(p\)-value comes from a wild cluster bootstrap with Webb six-point weights and 9,999 replications. As pre-registered, the twenty primary country contrasts carry a Holm step-down correction as a single family. The reason for the Holm step-down procedure is that I test 20 countries against the United States. With twenty contrasts each tested at the 5 percent level, about one would appear significant by chance even if advice were identical in every country. The correction keeps the probability of any false rejection among the twenty at 5 percent. Appendix Table A1 lists each pre-registered hypothesis with its test and outcome. The coefficients measure the causal effect of the prompt’s country, language, and framing on AI financial advice.

4.1Descriptive Patterns

I begin with descriptive facts about the advice that organize the results that follow. The models bunch their recommendations at a few round numbers within a narrow range, I can reject equality between countries, the differences between countries are one-sided (every survivor is negative) and modest (six points on average, at most eleven).

Starting with round numbers in the recommendations, Figure 1 plots the distribution of the recommended equity share, pooled across countries and models. Every response is a multiple of five percentage points, and 85 percent are multiples of ten. The modal recommendation is 60 percent equity, given in 31 percent of responses. In addition, the support is narrow. No response in 13,639 allocates less than 10 or more than 80 percent to equities. The bunching shows that the models answer a continuous allocation problem with a conventional round number. Choukhmane et al. (2026) also find round number bunching in AI advice, which they note departs from the more subtle prescriptions of life cycle theory. A portfolio rule would produce a continuous answer for at least some of the answers. Instead, the modal 60/40 split is the most common target allocation in retail portfolio advice, a first sign of the risk-questionnaire interpretation. This is not just because the models lack information about the investor, as 100 percent of the responses in the stated-investor arm are also round numbers. Ross and Lo (2026) report that between 89% and 100% of all allocation percentages are multiples of 5 without web search, concluding that “round-number anchoring is a deeply ingrained property of model behavior that web search alone does not fully override.”

4.2Country effects on the primary question

I now formally test whether LLM advice is the same across countries. The primary specification estimates country fixed effects from the following regression \[y_{imc} \;=\; \alpha_{m} + \gamma_{c} + \varepsilon_{imc}, \tag{3}\] where \(y_{imc}\) is the equity fraction recommended in repetition \(i\) by model \(m\) for country \(c\), the \(\alpha_{m}\) are model fixed effects, and the \(\gamma_{c}\) are deviations from the omitted U.S. baseline. The baseline regressions uses the age 40 version of the prompt in the local language.

The main finding is that advice is not the same everywhere, as shown in Figure 2 for all twenty contrasts.6 Appendix Table A2 reports each with its wild-bootstrap and Holm \(p\)-values. Because the Holm step-down controls the familywise error rate across the twenty contrasts, the seven contrasts that survive it reject equality of advice across the twenty-one countries at the 5 percent level. No joint Wald test is reported, because the model-clustered covariance of twenty country coefficients has rank at most twelve with thirteen clusters. Thirteen of the twenty contrasts clear the wild-bootstrap threshold before correction, and seven survive the Holm step-down: Brazil at \(-11.0\) percentage points, Italy at \(-8.6\), Poland at \(-7.4\), France at \(-5.4\), Spain at \(-4.4\), the Netherlands at \(-4.2\), and Mexico at \(-2.7\). In addition, every contrast that survives the correction is negative. Against a U.S. mean equity share of 0.57, the largest gap, Brazil’s eleven percentage points, moves $5,500 of the $50,000 stake from stocks to bonds. The seven significant contrasts average about six points. For comparison, the gender gap that Foltyn and Olsson (2026) document on a similar allocation question is under two percentage points. Country context moves LLM advice by several times the demographic gaps documented so far in the literature.

After the Holm correction, thirteen of the twenty contrasts fail to reject equality with the U.S. baseline. Six of those are individually significant on the uncorrected wild-bootstrap test but do not survive the step-down (China, Switzerland, Germany, Turkey, India, and Canada, ranging from \(-4.4\) to \(-1.1\) points). The remaining seven do not reject even before correction. Four of them have small positive point estimates (Australia, Japan, South Korea, and the United Kingdom). Finland and South Africa have small negative gaps, and Sweden sits at the U.S. level. The precision of these nulls differs across countries, because the models agree with one another far more in some countries than in others. In the three English-prompting countries (Australia, South Africa, and the United Kingdom) the 95 percent confidence intervals exclude gaps larger than about two and a half percentage points in either direction, which is $1,250 of the $50,000 stake. In Finland, Japan, South Korea, and Sweden the intervals are wider and reach between 3.4 and 5.7 points from zero, so the data cannot exclude gaps of the size estimated for Spain or the Netherlands.

4.3Language and local framing

I next run two within-country tests. One asks whether local framing shapes the advice, and the other separates the effect of the question’s language from that of the country name. The local-framing regression compares Versions C and B across the twenty non-U.S. countries at age 40, \[y_{imc} \;=\; \alpha_{m} + \gamma_{c} + \phi\,\mathbf{1}[\text{version}=C] + \varepsilon_{imc}, \tag{4}\] and the language regression compares Versions B and B-cross across the fifteen non-English countries, \[y_{imc} \;=\; \alpha_{m} + \gamma_{c} + \lambda\,\mathbf{1}[\text{language}=\text{local}] + \varepsilon_{imc}. \tag{5}\]

Asking in the local language rather than in English lowers the recommended equity share by 1.3 percentage points (\(\hat{\lambda} = -0.013\), wild bootstrap \(p = 0.007\), confidence interval \(-2.3\) to \(-0.4\) points). The language effect is statistically significant but economically small, an order of magnitude below the largest country effects. Naming the local instruments does even less. Replacing the generic vocabulary with local pension names, local government bonds, and local index funds moves the allocation by \(-0.7\) points, a result that is not significance at the five percent level. Figure 3 applies the same decomposition country by country. The country-name step is the English-version of the prompt minus the United States (Version B - US), the language step is the native language prompt minus the English prompt for each country (Version B minus B cross), and the local-context step is the native language prompt with local context minus the native language prompt (Version C minus Version B). The country-name step also changes the currency unit and the nominal amount, which move together with the country reference. In the large-effect countries (Brazil, Mexico, Poland, France, Italy, Germany) the country-name slab accounts for most of the gap. The language and local-context slabs are usually small, but they are noisier than the pooled \(\hat{\lambda}\) and \(\hat{\phi}\) and not uniformly negative. Japan is the clearest exception. A large positive language slab pushes its advice toward stocks and produces the panel’s largest positive total.

The size of the language effect helps distinguish between two views of how multilingual models answer a question. If the models retrieve advice the way they retrieve language-keyed knowledge, the language effect \(\lambda\) should be substantial. The same model often asserts different facts depending on the query language (Kassner et al. 2021; Qi et al. 2023). For example, if German queries were to draw on the bond-heavy conventions of German-language financial text, the effect of the language would presumably be large. If instead the models solve the task in a shared representation, \(\lambda\) should be near zero. Tracing studies in computer science find that language-specific processing lies in the layers nearest the input and output (i.e. the initial prompt and the explanation, which is always in the local language). More importantly, however, tracing studies also find that there is also a middle layers, where the LLM solves the task in a shared concept space lying closer to English (Wendler et al. 2024; Tang et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2025; Lindsey et al. 2025). Under such processing, two translations of the same question are nearly the same query in the middle layer, which support a small language effect. Indeeed, the estimate of \(-1.3\) points fits this second account, with the small residual consistent with the language-specific features that remain. The advice behaves like one policy applied across query languages rather than like language-segmented retrieval.

4.4The mortgage and inflation questions

I now turn to the two secondary questions. Figure 4 plots the country effects on all three questions side by side. For the two secondary questions, the advice differs more across countries and the variance decomposition reverses. Country accounts for 27 percent of the variation in mortgage advice against 15 percent for model. Model identity accounts for only 26 percent of the variation in inflation advice, whereas on the allocation question the shares are 6 percent for country and 70 percent for model. The secondary questions also pick up a different country signal from the portfolio allocation question. The countries that receive less equity on the allocation question (Brazil, Italy, Poland and France), are not the countries told to prepay (Japan, China, Switzerland, and Germany). Japan, statistically at the U.S. level on the allocation question, has the largest gap on both secondary questions. Across countries the allocation and mortgage effects correlate at \(-0.10\), while the mortgage and inflation effects correlate at \(0.80\).

On the mortgage question, the models recommend investing 10 to 29 percentage points less than in the United States in each of the fifteen countries outside the premise-acceptance group. All fifteen contrasts survive the Holm correction. Among the largest gaps are Japan at \(-28.6\), China at \(-22.7\), Switzerland at \(-21.6\), Germany at \(-21.1\), and Spain at \(-20.4\). Japan’s gap alone is more than twice the largest contrast on the allocation question. Part of the common gap reflects the United States itself, where deductible and freely prepayable mortgages favor investing. The ordering among the fifteen countries, however, spans a further 19 points and places the low-inflation economies at the bottom. For the five countries where the prevailing mortgage rates exceeded twice the prompted 4 percent at the filing date (Brazil, India, Mexico, Turkey, and South Africa), the contrasts are mostly null, and only South Africa (\(-2.4\) points) survives the correction.

On the inflation question, seven country contrasts survive the Holm correction, all negative, so in these countries the models recommend moving less of the savings out of the account than in the United States: Japan (\(-11.3\) points), China (\(-7.2\)), South Korea (\(-6.9\)), the Netherlands (\(-6.5\)), Italy (\(-5.0\)), Switzerland (\(-5.0\)), and France (\(-4.3\)). In addition to the share, I also ask the models to recommend an instrument to save in (see Appendix Table A4). Here we find the clearest evidence for localization: Equities are the primary instrument in 93 percent or more of responses in fourteen countries and in a clear majority in all but the highest-inflation economies. In Brazil, Turkey, and Mexico the models switch to inflation protection. In Brazil, inflation-linked bonds account for 86.7 percent of the recommendations, and in Turkey inflation-linked bonds and gold together account for 82.3 percent (66.0 and 16.3 percent). Mexico’s recommendations tilt the same way (58.1 percent inflation-linked bonds), and Poland’s are split (38.6 percent inflation-linked). The tilt appears even inside the English-prompting group, where models in the two higher-inflation members, India and South Africa, recommend inflation protection (11.5 and 16.3 percent, compared to under 3 percent in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia). Because these two countries share the baseline’s language, the switch tracks the country rather than the language of the prompt.

The country effects on the mortgage question sort into three groups by the local level of interest rates. In Japan and Switzerland, where policy rates were below one percent in 2024, the models recommend 25 points less investment than in the United States. In the five premise-acceptance countries, where policy rates exceeded six percent, the gap is 2 points. In the remaining thirteen countries the gap averages 17 points and is unrelated to the local rate (slope 0.07 points per point of the policy rate, \(p = 0.96\)). The pattern is consistent with models comparing the stated 4 percent with local rates only where the two differ widely.

4.5What does the advice track?

Financial advice from AI models differs across countries. I now ask whether the country effects track several country-level measures that an advisor may pick up on. The answer depends on the question. On the allocation question, the country effects track none of the characteristics I consider. On the mortgage and inflation questions, they track local interest rates.

I start with the willingness to take risks and financial literacy. I use data from the Global Preferences Survey from Falk et al. (2018) for risk taking and data on the share of adults classified as financially literate in the S&P Global FinLit Survey from Klapper et al. (2015). If AI models tailor their advice to country characteristics, they may plausibly infer that a country with a higher willingness to take risks should be given a higher equity share. Similarly, if models take financial literacy into account, they may give a higher equity share to a country with higher financial literacy. Financial literacy also proxies for a wide range of other characteristics, like general financial sophistication or high stock market participation (Rooij et al. 2011). I test these two predictions by regressing the country fixed effects from equation (3) on each measure.7 This is a pre-registered test for H7 and H8. Each test is a bivariate slope over the twenty non-U.S. countries, estimated with a wild bootstrap over countries. Both hypotheses sit at the second altitude of the registration. They are pre-specified in full and registered as descriptive, and a dated amendment froze the covariate vectors before I estimated any second-stage coefficient. Because that amendment post-dates the pilot, I keep these tests descriptive rather than confirmatory. Note that these tests are descriptive and that they ask whether the models impute national averages, not whether they should.

Appendix Figure A1 plots both relationships along with the statistical results. I find little support for either hypothesis. Panel A shows that the advice does not track measured risk preferences. The slope is flat and the wild bootstrap over countries does not reject zero. The point estimate is also economically small. A country that is one standard deviation more risk-tolerant on average than another country would receive \(2.8\) percent more equity. Even if we take the widest preference gap by country, that between Japan at \(-0.36\) and South Africa at \(0.97\), this would only predict a gap of under four points. At the same time, Japan receives advice statistically indistinguishable from the U.S. baseline, while South Africa, by far the most risk-tolerant country, receives slightly less equity than the baseline rather than more. Panel B tells the same story for literacy. Moving across the full literacy range in the panel, from 24 percent of adults in India and Turkey to 71 percent in Sweden, changes predicted advice by under three percentage points.

Measured preferences and literacy are not special in this respect. Appendix Table A5 regresses the country effects on each of sixteen country characteristics in turn, covering survey measures, income, the volatility and premium of world and home equity, policy, long-term and mortgage interest rates, inflation, pension replacement rates, and mortgage-interest deductibility, with a wild bootstrap over the twenty countries and a Holm correction within each question.8 I standardize continuous characteristics so that each slope is the change in the country effect, in percentage points, per standard deviation of the characteristic. Interest rates and inflation enter in logs. The English-prompt indicator is a dummy for English-speaking countries (United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, India and South Africa). These regressions were specified after the pre-registration was filed and are exploratory. Apart from H7 and H8, none of them is registered. For Question 1 on the portfolio allocation, no characteristic survives the correction for multiple hypothesis testing. The two largest unadjusted associations are the English-prompt indicator and the pension replacement rate, but neither is sigificant after the Holm step-down (\(p = 0.33\) and \(0.25\)). Overall, no country characteristic is significantly associated with the country coefficent on advice in Question 1.

Average advice lies within about six percentage points of the simple benchmark in Section 5.1. Regressing the country effects from Question 1 on the benchmark’s prescribed deviations from the United States gives a slope of \(\hat{\kappa}=0.13\) (Figure 5, Panel A). Dropping Brazil from the estimation lowers the slope to 0.03, dropping Turkey raises it to 0.21, and dropping any other country leaves it between 0.11 and 0.15. These results are all far from a slope of 1, which would indicate full pass-through of the currency risk, and is closer zero, which would indicate that all countries get the same advice as the United States. The wild bootstrap over countries rejects full calibration (\(p<0.001\) against \(\kappa=1\)) but does not reject the U.S.-default hypothesis (\(p=0.32\) against \(\kappa=0\)).9 With the benchmark, we can also (tentatively and cautiously) price the cost of following AI advice. Following the advice rather than the unhedged benchmark costs an investor in the median country only 1.6 basis points of the stake per year, about $40 over the prompt’s five-year horizon. The cost for an investor in Turkey is higher at 137 basis points per year.

However, the results for the mortgage and inflation questions are different. Interest rates and inflation positively predict the answer to the mortgage question. A one-standard-deviation higher log mortgage rate is associated with 6.6 percentage points more recommended investment, which survives the Holm correction (Holm \(p = 0.006\), \(R^{2} = 0.67\)), and the long-term rate and inflation each explain close to half or more of the cross-country variance. The models therefore draw on local economic conditions when a question involves a local interest rate, but not on the allocation question. Only part of this response is appropriate. Where inflation is high, the real return to prepaying the prompted 4 percent mortgage is low, so advising less prepayment is right. It does not follow that the savings belong in equities, because in the high-rate countries local bonds pay more than the mortgage costs. Across all twenty countries the advice tracks local rates but not its own benchmark (slope 0.5 points per standard deviation, \(p = 0.86\)).

Panel B of Figure 5 plots the mortgage advice against the benchmark of Section 5.2. In every calibration country the models recommend investing more than the benchmark prescribes, by 13 to 36 percentage points and by 28 on average. Where the benchmark lies below 0.40, from Japan to Sweden, the gap is nearly constant at 31 to 34 points, so the advice rises one for one with the benchmark. Above 0.40 the advice stops rising and the gap narrows to between 13 and 22 points. The United States is the exception. Its benchmark of 0.47 is among the highest, yet it receives the highest advice in the panel, 0.83, and the largest gap, 36 points. On the fifteen non-U.S. calibration countries, the calibration regression of Section 5.1 gives \(\hat{\kappa}^{Q2} = 0.23\) for the mortgage question, against \(0.07\) for the allocation question on the same fifteen countries. With fifteen countries neither slope is statistically distinguishable from zero, and the 95 percent confidence intervals of both exclude full calibration at one. In the five premise-acceptance countries the models lean against prepaying, with average advice at about 0.80, within two points of the U.S. level. The benchmark agrees that these households should not prepay, but it does not prescribe more equity. Because local bonds pay more than the mortgage costs, the savings not used for prepayment belong in bonds, so the benchmark is the allocation benchmark of 0.21 to 0.49. The advice exceeds it by 31 to 60 points, more than in any calibration country. Some models name the bond option explicitly. 4.6Robustness

I now describe several robustness checks. I first collect two pre-registered checks where I ask whether the results depend on the sampling temperature or on the size of the numerals in the local-currency amount.11 I had several other pre-registered checks, but these have nothing to act on. The collection produced no refusals, so the refusal outcome is constant and the zero-equity imputation changes no observation. The collection also also finished within two days, so estimating effect by week of collecting does not have any effect. After these checks, I also added several exploratory robustness checks where I first check whether the results change over time as new models are introduced, whether the English Instructions block affects the results, or whether allowing models to ask questions affects the cross-country differences. Finally, I also examine whether the answers depend on the country where I was when I sent the queries (Germany). The results are robust to these checks, which I now describe in more detail.

Temperature check.

Temperature is a sampling parameter that sets how much randomness a language model uses when it picks each word of its response. The next-token prediction at the heart of an LLM picks the next word based on a predicted probability. Model provider introduce some randomness in the token the model uses, which introduces some variation in the answers even with a similar prompt. At a temperature of 0, the model always chooses its most likely next word. Higher values make less likely words more probable. The main set of prompts use a sampling temperature of 0.7. The temperature check re-collects the primary cell at the temperatures of 0 and 1.0 for five countries (the United States, Germany, Sweden, Japan, and Brazil). Appendix Table A7, Panel A shows that temperature does not affect the results. The pooled mean equity share shifts by 0.3 percentage points at either bookend relative to the 0.7 baseline (wild bootstrap \(p = 0.55\) at temperature 0 and \(p = 0.40\) at 1.0), and the country contrasts barely move.

Numeral-magnitude.

The second robustness check addresses a numeral-magnitude confound. Version B states the stake as a round native-currency figure. The cross-country contrasts compare prompts whose numerals differ by orders of magnitude, such as 7,500,000 yen in Japan against 50,000 dollars in the U.S. baseline cell. If an AI advisor anchored on the size of the number, this could generate spurious country effects. For Sweden, Japan, and Germany I therefore re-collect Version B with the amount stated as $50,000, holding country and language fixed (Appendix Table A7, Panel B). The pooled shift is a tight zero (\(-0.1\) percentage points, wild bootstrap \(p = 0.84\), confidence interval \(-1.6\) to \(+1.4\) points). The one significant per-country shift is Japan, whose seven-digit yen figure raises the recommended share by 2.8 points relative to the dollar statement (\(p = 0.01\)). Japan’s country contrast is small and statistically null with native numerals (\(+1.2\) points) and remains small under the dollar statement (an implied \(-1.6\) points).

Purchasing power adjustment.

The main collection uses $50,000 converted at market exchange rates to the local currency. At purchasing-power parity, the stake is worth about twice the U.S. stake in Brazil, Mexico, Poland, and China, two and a half times in South Africa, and three and a half to four times in Turkey and India. If the models advise more caution on a stake that is large relative to local prices, part of these countries’ contrasts would reflect the size of the stake rather than the country.

On October 29, 2026, I restated the stake in the twenty non-U.S. Version B prompts as the purchasing-power-parity equivalent of $50,000, rounded in each prompt’s own notation, and re-collected the unchanged Version B prompts for all twenty-one countries, with twenty-five repetitions per model and cell. Appendix Table A8 reports the shifts. Cutting the real stake raises the recommended equity share by 1.0 percentage point on average (wild bootstrap \(p<0.001\), confidence interval 0.5 to 1.7 points). Each halving of the real stake adds 1.2 points (\(p<0.001\)). However, the effect is small relative to the baseline contrasts. For example, Brazil’s contrast moves from \(-11.5\) to \(-9.7\) points and Poland’s from \(-8.4\) to \(-6.1\). Stake size therefore accounts for between a sixth and a third of these contrasts. Moreover, the effect is not specific to emerging markets, since Spain’s contrast narrows by a similar 2.1 points.

Leave-one-out mean and vendor fixed effects.

The pre-registered leave-one-model-out sweep re-estimates equation (3) thirteen times, each time without one model. No contrast moves by more than 1.3 percentage points. Six of the seven contrasts that survive the Holm correction survive it in all thirteen samples, with a largest Holm \(p\)-value of 0.027. Replacing the thirteen model fixed effects with six vendor fixed effects, for comparability with Foltyn and Olsson (2026), leaves every country contrast essentially unchanged, although with six clusters the variant supports no formal inference.

Model drift over time.

To examine whether newer models give different results and to check the robustness of the answers over time, I re-asked one English slice of the primary cell on September 19, 2026 to a panel of twenty-one models. Fourteen of these models were new to the panel. The other seven carry over from the main panel under unchanged identifiers.

The main findings are robust. On the same English slice, model identity accounts for 70 percent of the variance in September against 78 percent in July, and country for 5 percent against 3 percent. Within each model, 77 percent of non-U.S. cells lie below the model’s own U.S. cell, against 67 percent in July. Six of the seven carried-over models return average advice within 2.5 points of July.12 The interested reader can explore the differences in the answers by model themselves on this website.

Caller location and routing.

Every query went through OpenRouter from Germany, which raises two concerns. First, the models may condition on the caller’s location, and second, that the routing of requests between hosts moves the estimates. Note first that the location is the same in every call, and that the APIs do not pass the location to the model. Each query is a single user message, the only text the vendors add is fixed (the current date for OpenAI and a system prompt for xAI). Asked what they know about the user, none of the thirteen models reports a location.

A direct test shows that the level does not move either. I sent the primary prompt for the United States, Germany, Brazil, and Japan to one model from each vendor in four blocks of 240 queries, routed through VPN exits in Germany and the United States. Moving the origin to the United States changes the recommended equity share by 0.4 percentage points. The shift does not differ across prompt countries (\(p = 0.23\)), and each model bills the same number of prompt tokens from both origins.

Translating the Instructions block.

Every prompt in the design wraps the translated question in the same English instructions. Since the models are sent the entire query, they may read the English instructions as a cue to anchor on an English-language answer, which would compress the country differences and understate the language effect. I therefore re-collected the primary cell on October 29, 2026 with the Instructions block rendered in the local language for the fifteen non-English countries. The six English-prompting countries received prompts identical to the main collection.

The main results are robust. The twenty country contrasts estimated on the new collection correlate at 0.93 with those of equation (3), eight of them lie below the United States after the Holm correction, and their range is 11 points against 12. Translating the instructions lowers the non-English cells by 1.4 points and the identical English cells by 0.6. The response is concentrated in two models. The two Gemini Flash models recommend about four points less equity when the instructions are in the local language, while the other eleven move by less than 1.5 points.

Allowing the models to ask questions.

A human advisor would ask questions before advising, a feature that the design denies the models. This raises the possibility that the uniform advice reflects a uniform lack of information rather than a uniform policy. I therefore re-asked the primary question in English for all twenty-one countries, where I first ask the models to list up to five questions it would want the investor to answer first. I also ask the models to state the direction in which each answer would move its recommendation, and gives the share it would recommend provisionally if the investor answered nothing further.

However, allowing the models to ask questions does not affect the results. Country means in the two arms correlate at 0.94 with a slope of one (Appendix Table A8, last column). Model identity accounts for 82 percent of the variance of the provisional share against 4 percent for country, the same ordering as in the main arm. The one difference is a level shift. Asked for a provisional number, the models recommend about six points less equity. The shift is uniform across countries and concentrated in the models with the highest equity shares.
