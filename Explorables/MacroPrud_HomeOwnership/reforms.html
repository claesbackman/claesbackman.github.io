9Large borrower-based reforms

The narrative index records changes in the regulatory stance, and several of the reforms the micro literature studies barely register in it (Section 5). This section asks whether those reforms are visible in national aggregates. I examine the introduction of loan-to-value and loan-to-income limits in the Netherlands in 2011 (Van Bekkum et al. 2024), the United Kingdom in 2014 (Peydró et al. 2024), Ireland in 2015 (Acharya et al. 2022), Sweden in 2016 (Bäckman et al. 2024) and Finland in 2016 (Eerola et al. 2023), with the remaining 23 countries as controls. Each reform had documented effects on the borrowers it targeted. Constrained first-time buyers in Ireland lost 20 to 25 percent of their mortgage access, high loan-to-income lending in the United Kingdom fell by about 15 percent, and affected buyers in Finland entered ownership later. I report the Callaway and Sant’Anna (2020) estimator, which compares each treated country with never-treated and not-yet-treated countries only. Pre-reform coefficients are level differences relative to the year before the reform, so a positive pre-reform coefficient means that the outcome was higher two or three years before the reform than in the reference year, relative to controls. Because the sample ends in 2017, horizon three is identified from the Netherlands and the United Kingdom and horizon four from the Netherlands alone. The two-way fixed effects event study of the earlier version also uses already-treated countries as controls (Goodman-Bacon 2021). Its estimates are in Appendix Table B31 and Appendix Figure A15.5 The earlier version also included Norway, Switzerland and Portugal, which are outside the 28-country estimation sample. Appendix Figures A4 and A5 retain them.

Figure 10 plots the event-time coefficients for real house prices, real household debt, the aggregate homeownership rate and the share of young households owning with a mortgage. Appendix Table B31 reports pre- and post-reform averages, and Appendix Table B30 reform-specific estimates, leave-one-out estimates and permutation inference. Credit and prices come first, because they determine how the ownership results can be read.

The reforms leave no common trace in aggregate credit or house prices. Real household debt moves by less than 2.5 percent at every horizon. The post-reform average is -0.6 percent with a standard error of 3.0, and the reform-specific estimates range from -11 percent for Ireland to +6 percent for the United Kingdom. Real house prices had risen by six to eight percent relative to controls over the three years before the reforms, and the average pre-reform coefficient is -6.4 percent with a standard error of 3.3. After the reforms the relative rise stops. The coefficients stay within five percent of the reference year at horizons zero to three, none of them significant, and the post-reform average is -1.7 percent with a standard error of 4.0. The decline of 14 percent at horizon four comes from the Netherlands alone, where the 2011 cap was followed by the trough of the Dutch housing bust and by the 2013 requirement that new mortgages amortize to remain tax deductible. The reform-specific price estimates range from -10 percent for the Netherlands to +12 percent for Ireland.

Two readings of the missing first stage are possible, and they are not exclusive. The first is statistical. Household debt is a stock, and lending for house purchase adds perhaps 5 to 10 percent to it each year. A cut of 20 percent in lending to constrained first-time buyers, who account for a fraction of that flow, lowers the stock by roughly one to three percent after four years, inside confidence intervals of about eight percent on either side. The second reading is that equilibrium adjustments undo part of the effect measured on treated borrowers. The micro studies compare exposed with unexposed borrowers, lenders or regions within a country. Any response common to the whole country, including the response of prices and of lending to unexposed borrowers, is differenced out by construction, so these estimates are relative effects and are silent on the aggregate.

The same studies record the adjustments that shrink the aggregate footprint. In Ireland, lenders reallocated mortgage credit toward higher-income borrowers and toward regions with slower house price growth (Acharya et al. 2022). In Finland, affected buyers entered ownership later rather than not at all (Eerola et al. 2023). In the segmented framework of Section 2.1, whatever demand shift remains is absorbed by prices rather than by the number of owners. These adjustments are part of how the policy works, and each drives a wedge between the effect on treated borrowers and the effect on national aggregates. The cross-country literature does find that borrower-based measures slow credit and house price growth (Kuttner and Shim 2016; Cerutti et al. 2017; Alam et al. 2019), so aggregate effects are not absent. They are smaller and slower than the exposure of treated borrowers suggests, and with five reforms and annual national data they cannot be separated from zero. The five reforms therefore do not supply the verified first stage that the average index lacks.

There is no common decline in the aggregate homeownership rate. The rate in treated countries had fallen by about 0.7 percentage points relative to controls between three years before the reforms and the year before them, and it rose after them. The average pre-reform coefficient is 0.78 percentage points with a clustered standard error of 0.65, and the post-reform average is 0.57 with a standard error of 0.33. Dropping one reform at a time gives post-reform averages between 0.04 and 0.78 percentage points. The reform-specific estimates are positive for the Netherlands and Ireland, close to zero for the United Kingdom and about -1 percentage point for Sweden and Finland, and the permutation p-value from reassigning the five treatment dates to random countries is 0.49.

The share of young households owning with a mortgage is lower after the reforms. The post-reform average is -1.31 percentage points with a standard error of 0.53, and between -0.9 and -1.7 when one reform is dropped. This is a margin on which the integrated framework predicts a decline. The local projections also show a decline in the low-income mortgagor share at the longest horizon. It does not survive closer inspection. The share had already fallen by about 1.1 percentage points relative to controls between three years before the reforms and the year before them, and the average pre-reform coefficient of 1.36 percentage points has a standard error of 0.73. The post-reform coefficients of -0.7, -1.4 and -2.7 percentage points at horizons zero to two continue that decline, and the coefficients of -0.9 at horizons three and four, from the Netherlands and the United Kingdom only, are smaller than the pre-reform decline. There is no break in the path of the young mortgagor share at the reforms. The permutation p-value is 0.29, and the reform-specific estimates range from 0.13 for the United Kingdom to -4.8 for Sweden. The homeownership rate of young households as a whole does not fall (post-reform average 0.30, standard error 0.75), and the share renting at market rates falls by 1.19 percentage points (standard error 0.46), the opposite of what elastic landlord conversion predicts. The two-way fixed effects estimates are -1.76 and -4.41 percentage points. The first has the opposite sign to the staggered estimate and the second is more than three times as large, and Appendix Figure A15 shows that the difference comes from comparisons in which earlier-treated countries serve as controls for later-treated ones.

Taken together, five well-documented borrower-based reforms were not followed by a common decline in aggregate credit, house prices or homeownership, and the one negative estimate, for young mortgagors, continues a relative decline that predates the reforms. The pre-reform paths show that the reforms came where house prices were rising and mortgaged ownership among the young was already falling relative to the rest of Europe. This is the endogeneity that the narrative index and the lagged controls in Section 5 address, and it means the comparison with never-treated countries is not clean. With five treated countries and heterogeneous institutions, the event studies are suggestive. What they do establish is a gap between the borrower-level and the aggregate evidence on the same reforms. The mechanisms behind that gap, reallocation by lenders, delayed rather than prevented entry, price adjustment and the dilution of flows in stocks, are the same ones that would keep the aggregate homeownership rate from responding to the average tightening. Section 10 makes the last of them explicit.
