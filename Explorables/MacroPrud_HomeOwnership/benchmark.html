10How large should the aggregate effect be?

A natural concern with the null results is that the aggregate homeownership rate may be too inertial to detect even substantial effects of macroprudential policies on the marginal buyer. The aggregate rate is a stock dominated by incumbent homeowners, while macroprudential policies primarily affect new mortgages. This section derives a simple stock-flow benchmark for the implied aggregate effect and compares it to the equivalence bounds estimated above and to the predictions of structural models. The benchmark is also the paper’s account of why borrower-level effects need not appear in the stock. The aggregate rate records the cumulated flow of first-time buyers net of exits, so a policy that delays entry, or excludes a minority of entrants for a few years, leaves it almost unchanged.

The aggregate homeownership rate evolves according to a simple accounting identity. Let H_t denote the number of owner households and N_t the total number of households. Ignoring household formation, the change in the homeownership rate is approximately \Delta h_t \approx \frac{\text{FTB}_t - \text{Exits}_t}{N_t}, \tag{2} where \text{FTB}_t denotes first-time entrants to homeownership and \text{Exits}_t denotes transitions from owning to renting. Repeat buyers do not enter the identity because they were already homeowners. Suppose a tightening in year t lowers first-time-buyer entry by a fraction \alpha of the baseline flow in every year from t on, and that excluded buyers enter D years later. Horizon h in Equation (1) spans h annual flows, from the policy year to h-1 years after it. At horizon h, the buyers excluded in the last \min(h, D) years have not yet entered and those excluded earlier have, so the shortfall is |\Delta h_h| \approx \kappa \cdot \alpha \cdot \phi \cdot \min(h, D), \tag{3} where \phi \equiv \text{FTB}_t/N_t is the baseline first-time buyer share of households. If excluded buyers never enter, the shortfall grows in proportion to the horizon. If they enter after a delay, it stops growing after D years, because from then on each newly excluded cohort is offset by an earlier cohort that enters. A delay therefore caps the shortfall at \kappa \alpha \phi D rather than removing it. Policies are announced during year t, so the five reported horizons cover between four and five years of exposure, and Equation (3) slightly overstates the shortfall at the fifth horizon.

The factor \kappa translates a shortfall in owner households into the person-based rate that EU-SILC reports (Section 3). For a household-based rate, \kappa = 1. In the person-based rate, a missing first-time-buyer household lowers the share of people in owner-occupied housing in proportion to its size relative to the average household, and does not lower it at all if its members remain in an owner-occupied parental home. So \kappa is the size of first-time-buyer households relative to the average household, times the share of excluded buyers who rent rather than stay in an owner-occupied parental home. Section 7.3 finds no rise in the share of young adults living with their parents after a tightening, so the second factor is unlikely to be far below one. I report the benchmark for \kappa = 1. A smaller value scales the shortfall down, and the exclusion consistent with a given bound up, in the same proportion.

Calibrating \phi requires data on first-time buyer flows. Across European countries, \phi ranges from approximately 0.8% to 1.8% per year, with a representative value near 1.3%.6 Sources: UK Finance for the UK; Central Bank of Ireland for Ireland; Statistics Netherlands for the Netherlands. A first-time buyer share of 1.3% implies that the average household enters homeownership roughly once every 75 household-years, consistent with a typical first-purchase age of 30–35 in an adult life of 50–60 years. For the policy-induced reduction \alpha, the micro literature gives two kinds of estimates. The first concerns the borrowers a rule targets. Acharya et al. (2022) document that credit-constrained first-time buyers lost roughly 20 to 25 percent of mortgage access following the 2015 Irish limits, and Peydró et al. (2024) find that the 2014 UK loan-to-income limit reduced high loan-to-income mortgages by approximately 15 percent. Because \alpha refers to all first-time buyers, these are upper bounds for it. The second kind of estimate is closer to \alpha. Eerola et al. (2023) estimate that the 2016 Finnish loan-to-value cap reduced transitions into homeownership by 17 percent among potentially affected buyers, which corresponds to an 8 percent reduction in all first-time apartment purchases. Bolliger et al. (2025) show that the share of Swiss renter households becoming owners fell from 3.4 percent per year in the five years before the 2012 measures to 3.0 percent in the five years after, a reduction of about 12 percent, which they attribute at least in part to tighter borrowing constraints.

Table B6 reports the benchmark at the fifth horizon with \phi = 1.3 percent. Panel A gives the shortfall for values of \alpha of 10, 20 and 30 percent and for delays of one, two and three years or no entry within the horizon. Panel B inverts Equation (3) and reports the largest \alpha consistent with the estimates, using the equivalence margin of 0.5 percentage points and the lower bounds of the 95 percent confidence intervals, which exclude declines larger than 0.17 percentage points without controls and 0.18 with controls.

The benchmark is an illustrative calculation, not an accounting identity that converts the estimates into excluded buyers. It holds the number of households fixed and treats the reduction in first-time-buyer flows as uniform across countries and years. It also sets a reduction in entry against the response to a one-unit change in the index, which does not measure the size of a restriction. With these limits in mind, three conclusions follow.

First, permanent exclusion of the size documented for targeted borrowers is not consistent with the estimates. Excluding 20 percent of first-time buyers permanently lowers the rate by 1.3 percentage points after five years, more than twice the equivalence margin. The margin rules out permanent exclusion of more than 7.7 percent of first-time buyers per unit of the index, or about 10 percent for the median tightening of 1.25 units, and the confidence intervals rule out permanent exclusion of more than about 3 percent per unit. If a one-unit change in the index reduced entry as much as the Finnish cap did and the excluded buyers never entered, the rate would have fallen by about 0.5 percentage points after five years. The structural models put the long-run response to tighter borrowing limits at 1.8 to 3 percentage points. Because those are long-run responses to specific changes in borrowing limits, the comparison concerns orders of magnitude and is not a test of the models.

Second, delayed entry reconciles exclusion of this size with the estimates. With a delay of one year, the equivalence margin allows values of \alpha up to 38 percent and the confidence intervals up to 13 to 14 percent. With a delay of two years, the corresponding values are 19 and 7 percent. Reductions in entry of 8 to 12 percent, the size estimated for Finland and Switzerland, are consistent with the confidence intervals if excluded buyers enter within one to two years. The aggregate rate is therefore informative about how long exclusion lasts as much as about how large it is. It rules out persistent exclusion of a few percent of first-time buyers per unit of the index, but not a delay of a year or less in the entry of a larger share.

Third, the margins where exclusion should appear first give the same answer. The evidence from Sections 7 and 9 is relevant here. The share of young households owning with a mortgage does not fall after the average change in the index; after the five large reforms it is about 1.3 percentage points lower than in control countries, from a base of about 40 percent of young households in those countries, but it had already fallen by a similar amount relative to controls before the reforms, and the permutation p-value is 0.29. A permanent reduction of 20 percent in first-time buyer flows would lower the young mortgagor share by several percentage points over five years, since young households account for most first-time buyers. The evidence on this margin is therefore consistent with the aggregate bound: exclusion of the size documented in the micro literature for individual reforms has not persisted on the scale required to move the stock. Household formation did not slow measurably either (Section 7.3). The results are silent on the marginal buyer’s welfare and on delays in entry of a year or less, which require micro evidence to address.

11Robustness

Pre-trends.

If changes in the index respond to past movements in homeownership, the local projections could pick up reversion rather than a policy effect. Appendix Figure A9 extends the cumulative response to three years before the change in the index, and Appendix Table B14 reports regressions of annual changes in homeownership one to three years before the index change on the index change. Without controls, the coefficients for three, two and one years before the policy are -0.03, -0.02 and -0.02 percentage points, none significant, and a joint test that the lagged changes predict the index change has a p-value of 0.45. With the full-coverage controls, the coefficients are -0.05, -0.07 and -0.07 percentage points, none significant, with a joint p-value of 0.55. These tests do not detect pre-trends, although they do not establish the exogeneity of policy changes.

Tightenings and loosenings.

The index treats a loosening as the negative of a tightening. Appendix Table B27 enters the positive and negative parts of the index change separately. The tightening coefficients are 0.09, 0.09, 0.04, 0.06 and 0.10 percentage points without controls and 0.08 to 0.17 with controls, with standard errors of 0.07 to 0.16, so the results above are results about tightenings. The loosening coefficients are based on 24 country-years. The loosening component enters with its negative sign, so a positive coefficient means that a loosening is followed by a lower homeownership rate. With controls the coefficients are positive at every horizon, between 0.02 and 0.55 percentage points, and significant only at the fourth. Without controls they range from -0.32 to 0.32 and are significant only at the fourth horizon, at the 10 percent level. The standard errors at the fifth horizon are 0.5 to 0.7 percentage points. For lending standard restrictions there are three loosening country-years, Greece in 2007, Portugal in 2009 and Latvia in 2014. With controls their coefficients are large, negative and significant, which would mean that these loosenings were followed by increases in homeownership of 0.8 to 2.4 percentage points per unit, but without controls they are small and not significant. Three events cannot support either reading. The data do not identify the effect of loosening, and for the overall index they give no indication that loosenings raised homeownership.

Inference.

With 28 clusters, asymptotic clustered standard errors may be unreliable. Appendix Table B28 reports, for the aggregate cumulative response, p-values and confidence intervals from a wild cluster bootstrap with Webb weights (Roodman et al. 2019), Driscoll-Kraay standard errors that allow for cross-sectional dependence, the range of coefficients obtained by dropping each country in turn, and population-weighted estimates. Without controls, the wild cluster bootstrap p-values are within 0.03 of the clustered p-values at every horizon. With controls they are higher, by up to 0.19 at the fifth horizon, so the bootstrap is the more conservative test. The bootstrap 95 percent confidence intervals for the specification with controls are [-0.06, 0.19] at the first horizon and [-0.21, 0.69] at the fifth. The lower bound of -0.21 implies that permanent exclusion of more than 3.3 percent of first-time buyers per unit of the index is ruled out, instead of 2.8 percent in Table B6. The upper bound of 0.69 means that equivalence at 0.5 percentage points at the fifth horizon with controls rests on the clustered standard errors, although the side it fails on is an increase in homeownership. Driscoll-Kraay standard errors are smaller than the clustered ones in the specification with controls, and up to 50 percent larger at the longer horizons without controls. Dropping any single country leaves every coefficient positive, with one exception: at the fifth horizon with controls, dropping Ireland lowers the coefficient from 0.18 to -0.04. Weighting country-years by population gives coefficients between 0.07 and 0.18 percentage points without controls and between -0.03 and 0.26 with controls. None of these variants shows a decline in the homeownership rate at conventional significance levels.

Regional shocks, trends and the post-socialist economies.

Year effects absorb shocks common to all 28 countries but not shocks common to a region, and the Central and Eastern European economies deepened their mortgage markets rapidly during the sample. Appendix Table B29 re-estimates the responses of the aggregate rate and of the young mortgagor share with region-by-year effects for Western and Northern, Southern, and Central and Eastern Europe, with country-specific linear trends, and without the 11 Central and Eastern European countries. No variant produces a negative coefficient that is significant at the 10 percent level. For the aggregate rate, region-by-year effects change the coefficients by at most 0.06 percentage points, and dropping Central and Eastern Europe gives coefficients between 0.01 and 0.23 percentage points. Country trends lower the fifth-horizon coefficient to -0.05 without controls and -0.01 with controls, with standard errors of 0.10 and 0.12. The 95 percent confidence intervals then extend to -0.26 percentage points, and equivalence at 0.5 percentage points still holds. The young mortgagor share shows no significant decline in any variant.

Randomization inference.

I reshuffle the index change across countries within each year 2,000 times and re-estimate the cumulative local projection without controls each time. Appendix Table B15 and Figure A10 report the results. The randomization p-values are 0.33, 0.41, 0.57, 0.33 and 0.64 at horizons one to five, and the p-value for the largest absolute coefficient across horizons is 0.62. The 97.5th percentile of the permuted coefficients rises from 0.12 percentage points at the first horizon to 0.25 at the fifth, which indicates the size of effect the design could have detected at each horizon.

Alternative policy database.

Appendix Figure A3 shows that the results for all groups are similar when the IMF’s integrated Macroprudential Policy (iMaPP) database, originally constructed by Alam et al. (2019), replaces MaPPED.

Control sets and house price source.

Table B10 compares the BIS and Eurostat control sets. Neither shows a decline. The BIS set, on 18 countries, shows positive and significant coefficients from the second horizon on. Real house price growth from Eurostat and from the BIS have a correlation of 0.99 where both are available, and the results are unchanged when the BIS index is used throughout.
