12Conclusion

Recorded changes in macroprudential regulation are not followed by detectable declines in homeownership rates in Europe. Across 28 countries and fifteen years, changes in a narrative index of non-counter-cyclical macroprudential actions leave the aggregate homeownership rate within half a percentage point of its previous level at all five reported horizons, and the estimates are precise enough to reject persistent aggregate effects of that size. Equivalence at the same margin holds at every horizon for the share living in homes owned with a mortgage and for the share of young households owning with a mortgage, and at four of five horizons for outright owners and for young households. The share renting at market rates does not rise, and neither does the share of young adults living with their parents. The estimates for low-income households are less precise, and the low-income mortgagor share falls by 0.48 percentage points at the longest horizon, significant at the 5 percent level. Around the large borrower-based reforms in the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, Ireland, Sweden and Finland, there is no common decline in the aggregate rate, and aggregate credit and house prices show no common movement either. The share of young households with a mortgage is about one percentage point lower after the reforms than in control countries, but it had already fallen relative to controls by a similar amount in the years before, the estimate is not significant under permutation inference, and the reform-specific estimates differ in sign.

Subject to the identification and measurement assumptions, the evidence rules out sufficiently large and persistent aggregate effects, while remaining consistent with temporary delays in entry and with effects concentrated on marginal buyers that do not accumulate in the stock. The results are consistent with the segmented-market view of Greenwald and Guren (2025), in which credit shocks move house prices and leave the homeownership rate unchanged because the supply of owner-occupied housing relative to rental housing is inelastic, and they are hard to reconcile with the aggregate magnitudes of 1.8 to 3 percentage points predicted by the central calibrations of models with elastic landlord conversion. They do not establish segmentation, because house prices do not respond in these data either. The index has a weak first stage on credit and house prices, a one-unit change in the index is a count of actions rather than a change in a borrowing limit of known size, and the large reforms do not produce a common movement in aggregate credit or prices in annual national data. The results are therefore also consistent with regulatory actions that had little effect on aggregate credit, and with borrower-level effects that were reallocated, delayed or absorbed by prices before they reached the stock of owners. Slow adjustment remains possible: five years may be too short for households delayed by a policy to appear in the stock, and the stock-flow benchmark shows that a delay of a year or two in entry moves the aggregate rate by only a few tenths of a percentage point. Delayed household formation would not be visible in the homeownership rate, but the share of young adults living with their parents does not rise after tightenings either.

For modeling, the results are a benchmark rather than a test. If the recorded tightenings constrained borrowers by amounts comparable to the model experiments, the aggregate homeownership responses in the central calibrations are too large, and the response would have to work through the timing of entry rather than through the stock of owners. The index cannot establish whether they did. Segmentation between rental and owner-occupied housing, whether through landlord technology, rental market regulation or the composition of the housing stock, is one way to obtain this. For policy, the results do not support the concern that macroprudential tightenings have reduced homeownership in aggregate. Over horizons of up to five years, the aggregate cost of recorded tightenings in terms of homeownership has been small. This does not mean that borrower-based measures have no distributional effects. The micro literature documents that they change who obtains credit, and in the five reform countries the share of young households owning with a mortgage was falling relative to the rest of Europe before the reforms and continued to fall afterwards. What the aggregate evidence shows is that these effects have not accumulated in the stock of owners. The micro studies document mechanisms that would produce this, including reallocation of credit by lenders and later entry by excluded buyers, and the stock of owners dilutes any change in the flow. The aggregate data do not identify which of these operated. The results do not show that relaxing borrower-based measures would leave homeownership unchanged, because loosenings are rare in the sample and their effects are not identified. They are silent on the welfare of the marginal buyer, on short delays in entry, and on other costs of macroprudential regulation, such as the reduction in household liquidity documented by Aastveit et al. (2023). Those questions require micro data, and the aggregate evidence here defines the magnitudes they should be reconciled with.
