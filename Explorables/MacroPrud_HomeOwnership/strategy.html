5Empirical strategy

This section describes the empirical strategy designed to identify how changes in macroprudential policies affect the homeownership rate in aggregate and for different groups. The analysis follows the literature on macroprudential policies (Fernández-Gallardo et al. 2025; Fernandez-Gallardo 2023; Kuttner and Shim 2016; Cerutti et al. 2017). I use a narrative approach to identify macroprudential policy shocks, and a local projection (LP) framework to estimate the effect of macroprudential policies on the homeownership rate.

5.1Identifying macroprudential policy shocks

To identify the effect of macroprudential policies on the homeownership rate, it is necessary to measure only exogenous components of macroprudential policy. In this setting, that involves identifying policies that do not systematically respond to changes in the homeownership rate or economic fluctuations that may drive changes in the homeownership rate. Overall, this is a challenge that arises because macroprudential policies are not introduced randomly, but instead often respond to the state of the economy. For example, macroprudential policies may be introduced in response to overheating of the economy. If policy tightening occurs at housing market peaks, the homeownership rate could decline for reasons unrelated to the policy.

To address this challenge, I follow the narrative approach of Fernández-Gallardo et al. (2025) and exclude policy actions with a counter-cyclical design. MaPPED records for each action whether the instrument has such a design, meaning that it tightens automatically when systemic risk intensifies and loosens when systemic risk decreases, or that its calibration is revised regularly in line with cyclical risk (Budnik and Kleibl 2018). I remove all policy actions with this characteristic. The filter removes instruments tied to the cycle by design. It does not remove an instrument without such a design that a regulator nonetheless introduced in response to a credit boom, so the remaining actions are not exogenous by construction. The identifying assumption is that, conditional on country and year effects and the lagged controls described below, the timing of the remaining actions is unrelated to other determinants of the homeownership rate.

A common approach in the literature is to include variables that could motivate the introduction of macroprudential policies, such as credit growth or house prices. Excluding actions with a counter-cyclical design addresses part of this concern, but the remaining actions may still respond to the state of the economy. To address this concern, I include control variables for the growth of private sector debt relative to GDP, the change in the real house price index, and the change in the short-term interest rate, alongside GDP growth per capita and inflation. I use two sets of credit and interest rate series. The first, used in the previous version of this paper, comes from the BIS and is unavailable for ten of the 28 countries, which cuts the with-controls sample by a third. The second comes from Eurostat and covers all 28 countries. Appendix Table B9 reports the estimation sample under each set, and Appendix C describes the series. I report the Eurostat set as the baseline with controls, because it keeps all 28 countries. Section 6 shows that the BIS set, on 18 countries, gives larger positive coefficients.

5.2Threats to identification

There are several other threats to identification, beyond the potentially endogenous implementation of macroprudential policies. I now discuss these in more detail.

Omitted variable bias.

If macroprudential policy moves together with the macro and housing cycle, there may be an omitted variable bias. For instance, loose monetary policy could increase housing demand or change expectations over future house prices, which may influence both the homeownership rate and the introduction of macroprudential policies. To address this concern, I include a number of control variables.

Measurement error.

Measurement error in policy indicators may mean that I assign a high weight to a policy that has a limited impact or scope. To address this concern, note that the weighting scheme of the MaPPED database allows me to give a lower weight to policies that have a limited impact or scope. In robustness checks, I also use another macroprudential policy database, and run results using no weighting. In unreported results, I find no differences in results.

Anticipation effects.

The MaPPED database contains information about both the announcement and implementation of macroprudential policies. If macroprudential policies are announced and implemented at different times, it may be the case that the implementation of a policy does not affect the homeownership rate, but the announcement does. To address this concern, I use the announcement date as the date of the policy. Overall, the difference between the announcement and implementation date is small, with an average difference of five months and a median difference of one month. Since I use annual data, the difference between the announcement and implementation date is unlikely to be a major concern.

Data frequency.

Statistical power is potentially a concern with annual data. However, unlike financial variables such as credit growth or house prices, which can respond quickly to policy changes, homeownership rates reflect long-term household decisions about tenure choice that are typically made infrequently and with considerable deliberation. Even if macroprudential policies do affect the attractiveness of homeownership, these effects may take several years to materialize as households gradually adjust their housing tenure decisions. For this reason, using annual data is appropriate in this setting.

5.3Empirical specification

I investigate the relationship between homeownership and macroprudential policies using local projections (LP) following Jordà (2005), Richter et al. (2019) and Coulier et al. (2022). I use this methodology as it enables me to estimate the effect of macroprudential policies over time, which is particularly relevant for a slow-moving variable such as homeownership rates. I estimate the following baseline model: \text{Owner}_{i,t+h-1}^j - \text{Owner}_{i,t-1}^j = \alpha_i^h + \gamma_t^h + \beta_h \Delta MaPP_{i,t} + \phi^h \Delta X_{i,t-1} + \epsilon_{i,t+h-1}, \tag{1} for h=1,\dots, 5. The dependent variable is the cumulative change in the homeownership rate for country i and group j from the year before the policy change to h-1 years after it, so that \beta_1 is the response in the year of the change and \beta_5 the level of the rate four years later relative to the year before. All estimates in the paper are cumulative responses of this kind; an earlier version estimated year-on-year changes at each horizon, which cannot be compared to the level predictions of the structural models or to the stock-flow benchmark in Section 10. The variable of interest, \Delta MaPP_{i,t}, is the change in the cumulative index of macroprudential actions. I include a vector of controls in X_{i,t-1}. Control variables are all lagged one year, and include GDP growth per capita, inflation, real house price growth, growth in private sector debt relative to GDP, the change in the short-term interest rate, and the level of the macroprudential index. \alpha_i^h and \gamma_t^h are country and time dummies that account for cross-country differences in the average homeownership rate and for common time trends. Standard errors are clustered by country; Section 11 reports wild cluster bootstrap, Driscoll-Kraay and randomization inference.

A one-unit change in the index corresponds to the activation of one new instrument, or to four changes in the level of existing instruments. In the estimation sample the index change has a standard deviation of about 1.5 and the median non-zero tightening is 1.25, so the tables also report the effect of a one-standard-deviation change and of the median tightening. The index does not measure the intensity of a restriction: an activation of a capital buffer and an activation of a loan-to-value limit enter with the same weight. Two features of the index matter for reading the results. First, the index change is positive in 135 country-years and negative in 26, so the estimates are driven by tightenings; Section 11 estimates tightenings and loosenings separately. Second, because actions coded as counter-cyclical in MaPPED are excluded, several of the reforms studied in the micro literature do not register: the Irish 2015 limits and the Finnish 2016 cap enter the index as zero, the Swedish 2016 amortization requirement as 0.25, the UK 2014 loan-to-income limit as 2.1 and the Dutch 2011 loan-to-value cap as 3. Appendix Table B8 lists every non-zero change in the index. The narrative index therefore measures recorded changes in the regulatory stance, and the results should be read as the average response to such changes rather than as the response to a credit shock of known size. Section 9 examines the large reforms directly.

I examine several measures of homeownership. All measures are expressed in percentage points of the relevant population. First, I use the aggregate homeownership rate. Second, I examine households with and without a mortgage, a distinction that is particularly relevant for macroprudential policies that directly target mortgage lending, and households above and below 60 percent of median equivalized income. Third, I examine young households and the share of young households owning with a mortgage, and fourth the composition of the rental market. For the first-stage regressions in Section 6.1, the dependent variable is the cumulative change in the log of house prices or credit between t-1 and t+h, so that the coefficients are percent changes.

Alongside conventional tests, I report equivalence tests and minimum detectable effects. A null result is only informative if the confidence interval is narrow relative to the effects of interest. The two one-sided tests procedure (Lakens 2017) rejects the hypothesis that the effect lies outside [-\delta, \delta] at the 5 percent level when the 90 percent confidence interval lies inside that interval. I report the test at \delta = 0.5 percentage points in the main tables and at \delta \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1\} in Appendix Table B11, together with the minimum effect that would be detected with 80 percent power. Section 10 maps each margin into the share of first-time buyers that a policy would need to exclude to produce it.
