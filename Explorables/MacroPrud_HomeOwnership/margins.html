7Young households and the flow into mortgaged ownership

The aggregate homeownership rate is a stock dominated by incumbent owners, while macroprudential policies act on new mortgages. If the policies exclude marginal buyers, the effect should be visible first among young households and among households who need a mortgage to buy. This section examines both margins.

7.1Homeownership of young households

The left panel of Figure 7 plots the cumulative response of the homeownership rate of adult-only households under 65, and Appendix Table B16 reports the coefficients. Without controls, the estimates range from -0.03 to 0.15 percentage points, none significant at the 5 percent level, with standard errors of 0.09 to 0.14. With controls, the estimates are positive except at three years, and the fifth-horizon coefficient of 0.37 is significant at the 5 percent level. Equivalence at \delta = 0.5 percentage points holds at every horizon without controls and at four of five horizons with controls. Appendix Table B17 and Figure A12 repeat the exercise for older adult-only households and for individuals aged 18 to 54, 25 to 54, 55 to 64 and 65 and over. The cumulative responses for the two younger age bands are between 0.04 and 0.14 percentage points at every horizon; the 65-and-over group has a small decline of 0.09 percentage points in the policy year, significant at the 10 percent level, but no significant decline thereafter. The integrated-market framework predicts a larger decline for young households than in aggregate. Neither appears in the data. The age bands available in the Eurostat tables are broad; narrower groups such as 25 to 34 would require the EU-SILC microdata.

7.2Young households owning with a mortgage

The right panel of Figure 7 turns to the share of young adult-only households who own with a mortgage. As discussed in Section 3, this is a stock that rises with first-time purchases and falls with repayment, ageing and changes in household composition; it is the available series most exposed to first-time-buyer entry, not a measure of the flow. Appendix Table B18 reports the coefficients. Without controls, the share is 0.15 percentage points higher in the year of a tightening, significant at the 10 percent level, and the subsequent cumulative responses are within 0.13 percentage points of zero. With the full-coverage controls, the cumulative responses are 0.08, 0.02, 0.02, 0.20 and -0.02 percentage points, none significant. There is no significant decline at any horizon. Equivalence holds at \delta = 0.5 percentage points at all ten horizon-specification pairs and at \delta = 1 percentage point at all of them; the minimum detectable effect is between 0.22 and 0.60 percentage points. Since young mortgagors are about 21 percent of young households, a given change in first-time-buyer entry moves this share by more than it moves the aggregate rate, so the absence of a decline here is more informative than the aggregate result about the entry margin. The share of young households owning outright does not respond either (Appendix Figure A11).

7.3Household formation

A tightening that prevents young adults from buying could also keep them in the parental home. This margin is hard to see in the homeownership rate. An adult child who stays with owner parents continues to count as living in owner-occupied housing, so a delay in household formation can leave the measured rate unchanged while access to independent ownership falls. I measure the margin directly with two further series. The first is the share of people aged 25 to 34 who live with their parents, from EU-SILC, the same survey as the tenure data. In 2017 it ranges from 3 percent in Denmark to more than 55 percent in Greece, Slovakia and Croatia, and its sample mean is 30 percent. The second is the average age at which young people leave the parental household, from the EU Labour Force Survey. Both series cover all 28 countries. Eurostat flags series breaks in both, and I set the flagged country-years to missing.

Figure 8 and Appendix Table B19 report the cumulative responses. The share living with parents does not rise after a tightening. Without controls the responses are between -0.19 and 0.08 percentage points, and with the Eurostat controls between -0.03 and 0.42, none of them significant. Equivalence at one percentage point holds at every horizon in both specifications, and at half a percentage point at every horizon without controls and at the first three with controls. Appendix Table B20 reports the share for other age bands and for men and women separately, with controls. Two of the 25 coefficients are significant at the 5 percent level, and they have opposite signs. The share of 25-to-29-year-olds living with their parents is 0.85 percentage points higher at the fifth horizon, and the share of women aged 25 to 34 is 0.27 percentage points lower at the second.

The average age of leaving home rises by 0.10 years at the third horizon with controls, significant at the 5 percent level, and by 0.04 years without controls, significant at the 10 percent level (Appendix Table B21). The other coefficients are smaller and not significant, and equivalence at a quarter of a year holds at every horizon. The two series are consistent with each other. The share living with parents falls by about four percentage points per year of age between the late twenties and the early thirties, so a delay of 0.1 years in leaving home would raise the share aged 25 to 34 by about 0.4 percentage points, which lies inside the confidence intervals above. The estimates rule out delays in leaving home of more than a quarter of a year, and the largest point estimate is about five weeks. The absence of a decline in the homeownership rate therefore does not reflect young adults who stay in the parental home instead of buying.

8The rental market

The two frameworks in Section 2 differ in how the rental market absorbs a credit tightening. With elastic landlords, households who can no longer buy rent instead, and landlords supply the units, so the share of households renting at market rates rises. With segmented markets, prices adjust and the composition of the rental stock is unchanged. Figure 9 brings the pieces together. The top row repeats the first-stage responses of house prices and private debt with controls; the bottom row shows the response of the aggregate homeownership rate and of the share of the population renting at market rates, both with the full-coverage controls. Appendix Table B22 and Figure A13 report all rental margins, in aggregate and for young households.

The share of households renting at market rates does not rise. The cumulative responses are -0.11, -0.34, -0.32, -0.19 and -0.32 percentage points; the first and third coefficients are significant at the 10 percent level. The share renting at reduced rates or rent-free moves in the opposite direction by similar amounts, so the estimates point to a small shift from market to reduced-rate renting rather than to more households renting from landlords at market rates. The standard errors for the rental shares are larger than for the homeownership rate, and equivalence at \delta = 0.5 percentage points fails at three of five horizons for market-rate renting. Among young adult-only households the estimates have the same signs and similar magnitudes. The margin on which the integrated framework predicts the clearest response, an increase in market renting, moves in the other direction, and the estimates are imprecise.
