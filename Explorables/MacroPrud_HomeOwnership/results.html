6Aggregate results

6.1First stage: house prices and credit

Both frameworks in Section 2 predict that a macroprudential tightening lowers credit and house prices, and the literature has documented both responses (Cerutti et al. 2017; Kuttner and Shim 2016; Alam et al. 2019; Fernández-Gallardo et al. 2025). Before turning to homeownership, I therefore ask whether the narrative index moves these variables in this sample. Figure 2 plots the cumulative response of real house prices and of private sector debt relative to GDP to a one-unit change in the index, with and without controls. The coefficients are in Appendix Table B12, which also reports household debt and the BIS credit series.

The average credit and price responses remain weak. Real house prices fall by 0.8 percent at the third horizon and 1.5 percent at the fifth without controls, but no coefficient is significant at the 10 percent level, and the longer-horizon response disappears once controls are included. Private sector debt relative to GDP does not respond significantly at any horizon. At the fifth horizon with controls, household debt relative to GDP falls by 1.8 percent and real household debt by 1.3 percent, both significant at the 5 percent level; the BIS credit series falls by 1.6 percent, significant at the 10 percent level. Restricting the shock to lending standard restrictions gives imprecise house-price responses of -0.5 to -1.6 percent without controls (Appendix Table B13 and Figure A7). Household debt relative to GDP falls by 1.7 percent at the fifth horizon without controls, significant at the 10 percent level, but the controlled estimate is not significant. Private sector debt relative to GDP, which includes corporate debt, rises significantly at some intermediate horizons after a tightening of lending standards.

The private-debt responses differ across tightenings. Past household credit growth does not predict tightenings of lending standards once country and year effects are included. Instead, the tightenings split into two groups. After 49 of the 122 tightenings of the overall index that can be classified, real household debt fell over the following two years. After the other 73 it kept rising. The second group is dominated by converging economies (Slovakia, Slovenia, Poland and Czechia) and by Sweden, where borrower-based measures were introduced during a structural deepening of mortgage markets that continued for years. The first group includes Hungary, the Baltic states, Croatia and the Netherlands, where tightenings coincided with the end of credit booms. Averaging across the two groups produces a first stage close to zero.

Two readings of the weak average first stage are possible. The narrative index measures the stance of regulation, and the average non-counter-cyclical action in MaPPED may be too small to move aggregate credit and prices in annual data across 28 countries. Alternatively, the effects documented in the literature are concentrated in the tightenings that reduced credit, and the index also counts many measures that did not. The rest of the paper addresses this in two ways. Section 6.3 re-estimates the homeownership response using only the tightenings after which credit fell. Section 9 examines the large borrower-based reforms directly, where the first stage is established in the micro literature.

6.2The aggregate homeownership rate

Changes in the macroprudential index are not followed by detectable changes in the aggregate homeownership rate at any horizon. Figure 3 plots the cumulative responses from Equation (1) without controls, with the Eurostat control set that covers all 28 countries, and with the BIS control set that covers 18. Table B10 reports the coefficients. Without controls, the cumulative response ranges from 0.06 to 0.11 percentage points, with standard errors between 0.08 and 0.12. With the full-coverage controls, it ranges from 0.07 to 0.18 percentage points, with standard errors between 0.06 and 0.18. The only significant coefficient is positive, at the fourth horizon with controls and at the 10 percent level. The lower bounds of the 95 percent confidence intervals are -0.12 percentage points at the third horizon and -0.17 at the fifth without controls, and -0.06 and -0.18 with controls. The BIS control set on 18 countries gives positive coefficients of 0.30 to 0.55 percentage points from the second horizon on, significant at the 5 percent level or better. In no specification does the homeownership rate fall. Table B3, the specification with controls used in the earlier version of the paper, now uses the full-coverage controls and coincides with Panel B.

The estimates are precise enough to be informative. Table B10 reports two one-sided equivalence tests at \delta = 0.5 percentage points and the minimum detectable effect at each horizon. Equivalence holds at every reported horizon both without controls and with the full-coverage controls; the p-value at the fifth horizon with controls is 0.042. The minimum detectable effect, the true cumulative effect that would be found with 80 percent power, is between 0.15 and 0.27 percentage points at horizons one to four and 0.33 to 0.50 percentage points at the fifth horizon. Appendix Table B11 varies the margin. Equivalence at \delta = 0.25 percentage points holds at horizons one to three without controls and one to two with controls, and fails at longer horizons; equivalence at \delta = 0.1 fails everywhere. The estimates therefore bound the cumulative effect of a one-unit change in the index at half a percentage point at all five reported horizons, and at a quarter of a percentage point at the first two to three horizons. Because the median tightening is 1.25 units, the corresponding bounds for a typical tightening are 0.625 and 0.313 percentage points. The baseline models in Garriga and Hedlund (2020) and Kaplan et al. (2020) predict responses of the homeownership rate to changes in loan-to-value and payment-to-income limits of 2.8 and 3 percentage points. The bounds are several times smaller than these predictions, but the comparison is inexact because the model experiments are specific changes in borrowing limits and the index change is a count of regulatory actions.

The absence of a decline in the aggregate homeownership rate stands in contrast to the effects of macroprudential policies on credit and house prices documented in the literature. Where those policies have effects, they are on the volume of credit and the pace of price increases. The share of households who own does not move.

6.3Tightenings that reduced credit

The weak average first stage leaves open whether the homeownership rate fails to respond to credit, or whether the index fails to move credit. To separate the two, I split each tightening of the index into two components according to what happened to real household debt afterwards. A tightening is classified as effective if real household debt fell between the year before and two years after the change in the index, and as not effective otherwise; tightenings in the last two sample years cannot be classified and are dropped. The two components enter Equation (1) as separate regressors, so that each coefficient compares country-years with that type of tightening to country-years without one. The classification conditions on an outcome observed after the policy, so it describes what happened to homeownership when credit did fall rather than identifying a causal effect of effective policy. A second definition, which classifies a tightening as effective when household debt growth decelerated relative to the three preceding years, gives similar results.

Figure 4 and Appendix Tables B23 and B24 report the results. The tightenings after which credit fell have a first stage of the size the literature documents. Real household debt is 1.1 percent lower after two years and 2.1 percent lower after four; the decline in the first three years is partly by construction, but it persists to the fifth year, when it is no longer constrained by the classification. Real house prices, which play no role in the classification, fall by 1.5 percent after two years and 2.1 percent after three, both significant at the 5 percent level. The tightenings after which credit did not fall are followed by rising debt and flat house prices. The homeownership rate does not fall after either component. After the tightenings that reduced credit, the cumulative response is 0.14 to 0.22 percentage points without controls, significant at the 10 percent level at the first horizon and at the 5 percent level at the fourth, and 0.18 to 0.30 percentage points with the full-coverage controls, significant at the 5 or 10 percent level at horizons one to four. The homeownership rate rose slightly in the years after these tightenings, which is the opposite of exclusion. The lower bounds of the 95 percent confidence intervals for this component are above -0.24 percentage points at every horizon. The share of young households owning with a mortgage does not fall after these tightenings, and the share renting at market rates does not rise. Appendix Table B25 and Figure A8 repeat the exercise for lending standard restrictions alone: real household debt falls by 1.2 to 3.2 percent after the 14 borrower-based tightenings that were followed by a credit decline, and the cumulative response of the homeownership rate is between -0.13 and 0.21 percentage points.

Because this split conditions on an outcome, I also split the tightenings on a variable fixed before the policy change: household debt relative to GDP in the previous year, above or below the sample median of 52 percent. Deep mortgage markets are where borrower-based limits are most likely to constrain buyers, but the split also separates Western European countries from most of the converging economies. Appendix Table B26 reports the results. Tightenings in countries with shallow mortgage markets are followed by declines in real household debt of 1.6 to 3.3 percent from the second horizon on, while tightenings in deep markets are followed by increases. This is the same pattern as the outcome-based split, and it says that the tightenings that reduced credit were mostly in the converging economies. The homeownership response differs across the two groups. In deep mortgage markets the cumulative response is positive, between 0.06 and 0.28 percentage points, and significant only at the fourth horizon. In shallow markets it is -0.12, -0.23, -0.24 and -0.30 percentage points at horizons two to five without controls, significant at the 10 percent level only at the third, and between -0.06 and 0.39 with controls, not significant. A continuous interaction of the index change with lagged household debt to GDP is small and never significant. The specification without controls therefore shows a decline in homeownership of 0.1 to 0.3 percentage points after tightenings in shallow mortgage markets, and the specification with controls does not. This is the only place in the cross-country evidence where a negative response of the homeownership rate appears. It is concentrated in the group of countries where credit fell, it is significant at only one horizon, and it is not robust to controls.

Taken together, the tightenings after which credit fell by about two percent were not followed by declines in the aggregate homeownership rate, and were followed by small increases in the specification with controls. In shallow mortgage markets, where these tightenings are concentrated, the specification without controls shows a decline of a few tenths of a percentage point. Neither pattern approaches the magnitudes the integrated framework predicts. Section 9 reaches the same conclusion from the five large borrower-based reforms, with the difference that credit and prices do not visibly move there. The two exercises are the paper’s closest approach to a credit shock of known effect, and both are suggestive rather than decisive, since the first conditions on an outcome and the second rests on five treated countries.

6.4Mortgagors, outright owners and income groups

Next, I examine homeownership rates for different groups of households. A concern for policymakers is that macroprudential policies affect some groups more than others, in particular low-income households and households who need a mortgage. Figure 5 shows the cumulative responses for low-income and high-income households and for households with and without mortgages, with the full-coverage controls. Table B4 reports the coefficients. The aggregate mortgagor share does not show a significant decline, but low-income mortgagors do at the longest horizon. The share living in homes owned with a mortgage moves by less than 0.14 percentage points in absolute value at every horizon, and equivalence at 0.5 percentage points holds at every horizon. The low-income results are less precise. Low-income households are defined by income below 60 percent of the median in the survey year, which is a small and changing group, and the standard errors are up to three times larger than for the aggregate rate. The cumulative response of low-income homeownership ranges from -0.10 to 0.18 percentage points, and the response of low-income mortgagors from -0.48 to 0.07, with the fifth-horizon coefficient of -0.48 significant at the 5 percent level. Equivalence at 0.5 percentage points fails at two horizons for low-income homeownership and at one horizon for low-income mortgagors. These estimates rule out large declines but not moderate ones, and they should not be read as precise nulls. Low income in the survey year is also not a measure of proximity to a borrowing constraint, since the group includes many retired outright owners.

6.5Lending standard restrictions

The next set of results uses only lending standard restrictions. These restrictions, which include LTV and debt-service-to-income (DSTI) limits, are the macroprudential tools that act directly on household borrowing, and they are the tools the structural models study. I construct an index from these actions alone and estimate Equation (1) with it. The index has 30 non-zero tightenings in the contemporaneous estimation sample, so the estimates are less precise than for the overall index. Appendix Table B13 reports the cumulative response of the aggregate homeownership rate: between -0.26 and 0.18 percentage points without controls and between -0.01 and 0.23 with controls, with no coefficient significant and standard errors of 0.08 to 0.34. Figure 6 shows the responses by group. For low-income households the response is small and negative in the first year and positive afterwards, significant at the 10 or 5 percent level at some horizons. Lending standard restrictions are not followed by declines in homeownership at any horizon, but the confidence intervals for this index are roughly twice as wide as for the overall index.
