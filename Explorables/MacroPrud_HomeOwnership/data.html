3Data and variables

The empirical analysis combines homeownership and tenure data from the European Union Statistics on Income and Living Conditions (EU-SILC), macroprudential policy actions from the Macroprudential Policies Evaluation Database (MaPPED) provided by the European Central Bank (Budnik and Kleibl 2018), macroeconomic, house price, debt and interest rate series from Eurostat, and credit and policy rate data from the Bank for International Settlements (BIS). All data are collected or collapsed to the annual level to match the frequency of the homeownership data. Below I describe the homeownership and macroprudential data and the criteria used for selecting time periods and countries. The macro data are described in Appendix C.

3.1Homeownership and tenure data

I collect homeownership data from EU-SILC. Aggregate homeownership data are available for 35 countries, with the earliest series starting in 2003. In addition to the aggregate homeownership rate, I collect the shares living in homes owned with or without a mortgage, the share renting at market rates and at reduced rates or rent-free, and all categories for households above or below 60 percent of median equivalized income. Eurostat publishes these series as distributions of the population by the tenure status of the household each person lives in. The homeownership rate is therefore the share of people living in owner-occupied dwellings, not the share of households that own. The two differ because owner and renter households differ in size, and because an adult child living with owner parents counts as living in owner-occupied housing. In the empirical analysis, I use these shares in levels, in percentage points of the population, and refer to them as homeownership rates. Section 10 returns to the difference when translating the estimates into first-time-buyer flows.

To examine the marginal buyer, I use two further EU-SILC breakdowns. The first is tenure by household composition, which separates people in adult-only households in which all members are under 65 from people in adult-only households with at least one member aged 65 or over. For each group I compute the share owning, the share owning with a mortgage, the share owning outright, and the share renting at market rates and at reduced rates, again as shares of the people in the group. For brevity I refer to these as the homeownership rate of young households and the share of young households owning with a mortgage. Households with dependent children are not included in either group, so the “young” group consists of singles and couples without children under 65. The second breakdown is tenure by age of the individual, which provides ownership rates for individuals aged 18 to 54, 25 to 54, 55 to 64 and 65 and over. Eurostat reports these rates separately for nationals and foreign citizens, and I use the rates for nationals. Both breakdowns are available for the same 28 countries and years as the aggregate series. The share of young adult-only households owning with a mortgage is a stock, not a flow. It rises when a young household buys with a mortgage, and it falls when mortgagors repay, age past 65, have children and leave the group, or sell. Among the available cross-country series it is the one most exposed to first-time-buyer entry, and I use it as a proxy for that margin with these limitations in mind. It also excludes families with dependent children, who account for a substantial share of first-time buyers.

3.2Macroprudential policies

I use the Macroprudential Policies Evaluation Database to construct a country-specific macroprudential policy index. The database contains 1,417 policy actions undertaken in 28 countries after 1995.3 The database contains several policies enacted before 1995, if they are still in place in 1995. In total, the database contains 1,925 policies. The database covers 11 policy instruments including capital buffers, lending standards restrictions, leverage ratios, and liquidity requirements. The last update was the fourth quarter of 2017, which marked the end of the sample period.

The MaPPED data have several advantages compared to other datasets (Fernández-Gallardo et al. 2025). The data are based on surveys of national regulators, allowing comparison of policy actions across countries. The data contain information about each policy action, including announcement and implementation dates, and whether the policy involved tightening, loosening or ambiguous effects. Importantly for identification, the survey asks if the policy had a counter-cyclical motivation or design.

Following Fernández-Gallardo et al. (2025) and Fernandez-Gallardo (2023), I construct an overall index of macroprudential policies using all non-countercyclical policies. I use the announcement date to assign a value to each policy, using a positive value for tightening, a negative value for loosening and a zero for ambiguous actions.4 The implementation date is also noted in the dataset. The median difference between announcement and implementation is 1 month, and the average difference is 5 months. Following (Meuleman and Vander Vennet 2020), I account for differences in scope between new and existing policies by assigning different weights. The weighting scheme gives higher weight to activations and deactivations, compared to changes in scope or level. The weighting scheme is provided in Table B1.

For each country, I then cumulate the signed and weighted announcements over time into an index. The variable of interest is the year-over-year change in the index, denoted \Delta MaPP_{i,t}, which equals the net weighted sum of the actions announced in year t.

3.3Sample selection and summary statistics

The sample selection is guided by data availability for homeownership rates and macroprudential policies. The final sample includes 28 countries. Homeownership data begins in 2003, but with many missing observations. 4Homeownership rates and macroprudential policies, 2010–2017

Homeownership rates move slowly. Figure 1 plots the homeownership rate in 2017 against the rate in 2010 for each country, and Appendix Figure A1 plots the full time series. The rate declined in most countries between 2010 and 2017, by 1.25 percentage points on average, and the decline was larger in the countries that used fewer macroprudential policies. A regression of the change in homeownership on the change in the macroprudential index between 2010 and 2017 gives a positive and insignificant coefficient of 0.2. Cross-country differences in levels are large and persistent: rates above 80 percent in Romania, Lithuania and Slovakia reflect the sale of state housing to tenants after 1990 (Broulı́ková et al. 2020), and rates near 50 percent in Germany and Austria reflect rental market institutions and preferences (Kaas et al. 2021; Huber and Schmidt 2022). These differences are absorbed by country fixed effects in the analysis below; the persistence of the series is why the empirical design focuses on changes over horizons of up to five years.
