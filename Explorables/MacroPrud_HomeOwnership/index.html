Macroprudential policies and homeownership rates

Claes Bäckman
Leibniz Institute for Financial Research SAFE

Abstract

Macroprudential policies are a key tool of financial regulators, but concerns persist that they restrict access to homeownership. I examine this concern for 28 European countries over 2003 to 2017, using a narrative index of macroprudential actions and event studies around five large borrower-based reforms. Recorded actions are not followed by detectable declines in aggregate homeownership or in ownership among young households, and the share of young adults living with their parents does not rise. The aggregate estimates establish equivalence within half a percentage point at all five reported horizons. The low-income mortgagor share, however, falls by about half a percentage point at the longest horizon. The aggregate rate does not fall after the large reforms either, and neither actions nor reforms leave a common trace in aggregate credit or house prices, although the micro literature documents large effects on targeted borrowers. Borrower-level effects need not aggregate, because lenders reallocate credit, excluded buyers enter later, prices absorb part of the demand shift, and the homeownership stock dilutes the flow of first-time buyers. The results do not support concerns that macroprudential policies have reduced homeownership in aggregate, while leaving open effects on who obtains credit and on the timing of entry.

JEL: R21, G28 · Keywords: macroprudential policy, homeownership, household credit, housing affordability

The dataHomeownership rates in 28 European countries, 2003–2017

Each grey line is one country; the orange line is the cross-country average. Click a line to highlight a country. The dots on a highlighted line mark the years in which that country changed its macroprudential stance (orange: net tightening, green: net loosening). Homeownership rates move slowly, and the paper asks whether these policy changes are followed by any movement at all.

How to read this site

  • The paper is split into the pages listed on the left, in the paper's own section numbering. Every figure is drawn from the estimates themselves and can be switched to a table; every table is the paper's table.
  • Footnotes open inline when you click the small numbered buttons. Citations link to the reference list.
  • The robustness explorer contains every specification that could feasibly be run on these data: several thousand local projections across policy indices, control sets, fixed effects, samples, weights and dependent-variable definitions, plus leave-one-out estimates, alternative inference and event-study variants. Each figure links to the explorer with its own specification preselected.
  • The replication page has the code, the data files and a prompt for asking an AI assistant about the paper.
