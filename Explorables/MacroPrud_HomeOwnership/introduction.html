1Introduction

Recorded changes in macroprudential regulation are not followed by detectable changes in homeownership rates. This paper documents that result for 28 European countries over 2003–2017, and shows that the estimates are precise enough to rule out persistent aggregate effects larger than half a percentage point per unit of the policy index. That bound is several times smaller than the responses to tighter borrowing limits in a widely used class of structural housing models, although the index and the model experiments are not measured in the same units. The question matters because borrower-based measures such as loan-to-value and debt-service-to-income limits have become standard tools of financial regulation, and because they are widely believed to come at a cost. Macroprudential policies appear successful in limiting credit growth and house price growth (Arena et al. 2020), but the concern is that they exclude first-time buyers and low-income households from homeownership. Several countries have relaxed loan-to-value limits or introduced exemptions for first-time buyers on these grounds. Yet the empirical literature on macroprudential policy focuses almost exclusively on credit and house prices. Araujo et al. (2024) collect 6,000 estimates from 58 studies of the effects of macroprudential policies and do not mention homeownership once.1 A large number of studies have examined the impact of macroprudential policies on credit and house price growth (see Galati and Moessner 2013, 2018; Araujo et al. 2024, for an overview). Kuttner and Shim (2016) find significant negative effects of policy tightening on housing credit and house price growth. Lim et al. (2011) show that various macroprudential tools affect the cyclicality of private sector credit and leverage. Alam et al. (2019) demonstrate that tighter Loan-to-Value (LTV) constraints have non-linear effects on credit. Igan and Kang (2011) find that transactions and house prices drop significantly after debt-to-income (DTI) and LTV tightening. Other studies include Cerutti et al. (2017), Akinci and Olmstead-Rumsey (2018), and Poghosyan (2020). Tzur-Ilan (2023) show that higher LTV limits resulted in borrowers choosing more affordable housing units. Laufer and Tzur-Ilan (2021) find that higher risk-weights on high-LTV mortgages slow house price growth. Peydró et al. (2024) show that caps on high loan-to-income mortgages reduced credit supply and house price growth. To my knowledge, none of these studies have linked macroprudential policies to homeownership rates.

I combine the Macroprudential Policies Evaluation Database (MaPPED) of the European Central Bank (Budnik and Kleibl 2018) with homeownership data from the European Union Statistics on Income and Living Conditions (EU-SILC). Following the narrative approach of Fernández-Gallardo et al. (2025), I build a country-level index from policy actions that were not taken with a counter-cyclical motive, and estimate local projections of the cumulative change in the homeownership rate on changes in this index over horizons of one to five years. The sample of 28 countries is larger than in most cross-country studies of macroprudential policy, and the with-controls specifications retain all 28 countries. The cumulative response of the aggregate homeownership rate to a one-unit change in the index is between 0.05 and 0.18 percentage points at every horizon, with no significant decline. The 95 percent confidence intervals exclude declines larger than 0.12 percentage points at the third reported horizon and larger than 0.18 percentage points at the fifth. Two one-sided equivalence tests reject cumulative effects larger than 0.5 percentage points in absolute value at all five reported horizons, both with and without controls. The minimum detectable effect is between 0.15 and 0.50 percentage points. For comparison, the baseline calibrations of Kaplan et al. (2020), Garriga and Hedlund (2020) and Castellanos et al. (2024) imply aggregate responses to changes in borrowing constraints of 1.8 to 3 percentage points. A one-unit change in the index is the activation of one instrument; the median tightening in the estimation sample is 1.25 units, so the bounds for a typical tightening are 25 percent wider.

The paper makes three contributions. The first is the aggregate result itself, together with an explicit account of what it can and cannot detect. Macroprudential policies act on new mortgages, and the homeownership rate is a stock dominated by incumbent owners, so a small aggregate response is compatible with a large effect on the marginal buyer. Section 10 makes this precise with a stock-flow benchmark. With roughly 1.3 percent of households entering ownership each year, a policy that permanently excludes 20 percent of first-time buyers lowers the aggregate rate by 1.3 percentage points over five years, which the estimates for a one-unit change in the index rule out. The equivalence margin of 0.5 percentage points corresponds to a permanent exclusion of about 8 percent of first-time buyers, or to an exclusion of 19 percent that lasts two years. Micro estimates for Finland and Switzerland put the reduction in first-time entry after borrower-based tightenings at 8 to 12 percent. The aggregate rate is therefore informative about persistent exclusion of this size, and much less informative about delays in entry of a year or so. The share of young adults living with their parents does not rise after tightenings either, so the stable rate does not reflect delayed household formation.

The second contribution is evidence on the margins where the marginal buyer should be visible. Using EU-SILC tenure data by household composition and by age, I estimate the same local projections for the homeownership rate of adult-only households under 65, of individuals aged 18 to 54 and 25 to 54, and for the share of young households who own with a mortgage. The last series is a stock, but among the available cross-country series it is the one most exposed to first-time-buyer entry. None of these margins falls after a tightening. The young mortgagor share moves by less than 0.3 percentage points at every horizon, with no negative coefficient that is distinguishable from zero. In models where landlords convert housing between segments, a credit tightening raises the share of households renting at market rates. In the data this share falls slightly, by 0.1 to 0.3 percentage points, and the estimates are imprecise.

The third contribution is to ask whether the borrower-based reforms studied in the micro literature are visible in national aggregates. I estimate the staggered difference-in-differences estimator of Callaway and Sant’Anna (2020) around the introduction of loan-to-value and loan-to-income limits in the Netherlands (2011), the United Kingdom (2014), Ireland (2015), Sweden (2016) and Finland (2016).2 Van Bekkum et al. (2024) for the Netherlands, Peydró et al. (2024) for the United Kingdom, Acharya et al. (2022) for Ireland, Bäckman et al. (2024) for Sweden and Eerola et al. (2023) for Finland. These reforms had large effects on the borrowers they targeted, but they leave no common trace in aggregate household credit, house prices or the aggregate homeownership rate. The share of young households owning with a mortgage is 1.3 percentage points lower after the reforms than in control countries, but it had been falling relative to controls by a similar amount before them, the estimate is not significant under permutation inference, and the reform-specific estimates differ in sign. The gap between the borrower-level and the aggregate evidence is informative in itself. The micro studies identify relative effects within a country, which difference out any common response, and they document that lenders reallocated credit toward unconstrained borrowers and that excluded buyers entered later. Effects estimated on treated borrowers therefore need not aggregate, and the same mechanisms would keep the aggregate homeownership rate from responding to the average tightening. Two-way fixed effects event studies, on which the earlier version of this paper and much of the literature rely, give a negative aggregate estimate where the staggered estimator gives a positive one, and a young mortgagor estimate more than three times larger. With five treated countries these event studies are suggestive rather than decisive.

Two caveats shape how the results should be read. First, the macroprudential index has a weak first stage in this sample. Real house prices fall by about 0.8 percent at the third reported horizon after a one-unit tightening in the specification without controls, but the estimate is imprecise and not robust to controls. Private debt does not respond significantly, although household debt falls at the longest horizon with controls. The five large borrower-based reforms in Section 9 do not supply a stronger first stage, since aggregate credit and house prices show no common movement around them either. The average conceals two groups of tightenings: after 49 of 122, real household debt fell over the following two years, while after the rest it kept rising, mostly in converging economies with deepening mortgage markets. The homeownership rate does not fall after either group, but this split conditions on an outcome observed after the policy and is descriptive. A split on a predetermined variable, the depth of the mortgage market before the policy change, shows that the tightenings that reduced credit were concentrated in countries with shallow mortgage markets. In those countries the homeownership rate falls by 0.1 to 0.3 percentage points in the specification without controls, significant at the 10 percent level at one horizon, and does not fall in the specification with controls. Second, the paper does not identify the mechanism behind the results. Section 2 uses the segmented-market framework of Greenwald and Guren (2025) and the integrated-market framework of Kaplan et al. (2020) to organize the predictions, and Table 1 lists the margins on which they differ. The evidence is consistent with the segmented framework on every margin and hard to reconcile with the aggregate magnitudes of the integrated framework’s central calibrations. A null response is also consistent with an index that has little effect on credit, with delayed rather than prevented entry, with exemptions that protect marginal buyers, and with effects that differ in sign across countries.

The paper contributes to the literature on credit and housing markets. Many papers estimate the causal effect of credit and interest rates on house prices (Glaeser et al. 2012; Adelino et al. 2025; Favara and Imbs 2015; Loutskina and Strahan 2015; Di Maggio and Kermani 2017; Mian and Sufi 2019), but few examine homeownership. Greenwald and Guren (2025) show for US regions that credit shocks move house prices but not homeownership rates, and interpret the result through segmentation between rental and owner-occupied housing. The results here extend that finding to a cross-country setting and to a different source of variation in credit conditions. The paper also contributes to the empirical literature on macroprudential policy summarized in the footnote above, which has established effects on credit and house prices but not examined the homeownership rate, and to a growing body of micro evidence on borrower-based measures (Acharya et al. 2022; Peydró et al. 2024; Van Bekkum et al. 2024; Eerola et al. 2023; Bolliger et al. 2025; Aastveit et al. 2023; Tzur-Ilan 2023). That literature documents effects on transactions, leverage and the timing of purchases. The aggregate evidence here shows that these effects have not translated into changes in who owns.

The results speak to policy in a limited way. They indicate that recorded tightenings of macroprudential regulation have not been followed by declines in aggregate homeownership over horizons of up to five years, so the case for relaxing borrower-based measures cannot rest on the homeownership rate. They do not show what loosening would do: only 26 of the 161 non-zero changes in the index are net loosenings, and their coefficients are imprecise and inconsistent across horizons. The results are also silent on the welfare of the marginal buyer, on short delays in entry, and on other costs of macroprudential regulation, such as reductions in household liquidity (Aastveit et al. 2023).
