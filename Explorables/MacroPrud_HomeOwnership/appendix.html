COnline Appendix: Data

C.1Macroeconomic data

I collect data from the national accounts for each country at the quarterly and annual level. The data is collected from Eurostat, unless otherwise indicated. From national accounts, I collect real GDP, final consumption expenditure, gross capital formation, total employment, and population. All variables are available at current prices or at purchasing power standard per capita. I also collect data on gross capital formation (divided into total fixed assets and total construction) in gross terms and as a share of GDP, and finally I collect the harmonised Indices of Consumer Prices (HICP) as a measure of inflation.

C.2Credit, interest rates and house price data

I collect credit data from the BIS total credit statistics. The dataset includes credit data for a number of different countries and series, including credit to the non-financial sector and the government sector. The data is divided into two series on the lending side: Total credit and Bank credit. Total credit comprises lending from domestic banks, other domestic financial corporations, non-financial corporations and non-residents, whereas bank lending only includes credit from domestic banks to the private non-financial sector. On the borrowing side, total credit is broken down into the government sector and private non-financial sector, where the latter is further broken down into non-financial corporations and households (including non-profit institutions serving households).

The BIS total credit statistics do not cover ten of the 28 countries in the sample (Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Romania, Slovakia and Slovenia). For the specifications with control variables I therefore use Eurostat’s private sector debt series (tipspd20), which measures the consolidated stock of loans and debt securities of non-financial corporations, households and non-profit institutions serving households as a share of GDP, and the corresponding household debt series (tipspd22). Both series cover all EU member states from 1995. Eurostat no longer publishes these series for the United Kingdom, for which I use the BIS credit-to-GDP series for the private non-financial sector and for households. Annual growth rates of the BIS and Eurostat series have a correlation of 0.58 where both are available. Tables that use the BIS credit series are labeled as such.

House prices are measured with Eurostat’s harmonized house price index (prc_hpi_a, all dwellings), deflated by the HICP and indexed to 2010. Eurostat does not publish an index for Greece, for which I use the BIS real residential property price index. For a few countries the Eurostat index starts after 2005 (Italy in 2010, Austria and Poland in 2008), and I extend it backward using the growth rate of the BIS index. Where both indices are available, their annual growth rates have a correlation of 0.99.

Short-term interest rates are measured with the 3-month money market rate from Eurostat (irt_st_a). For euro area members the euro area rate applies from the year of adoption, and national rates from Eurostat’s historical series (irt_h_mr3_a) apply before adoption. Eurostat discontinued the national money market series for non-euro members after 2014, and for 2015 onward I use the BIS central bank policy rate for Croatia, Czechia, Hungary, Poland and Romania. Bulgaria, which operates a currency board, has no rate for these years. The interest rate enters the regressions as a first difference in percentage points. The BIS policy rate series, used in the robustness specifications, is missing for the same ten countries as the BIS credit data because it is only compiled for countries in the BIS credit statistics. The annual changes in the two rate series have a correlation of 0.97.7 For more information on the BIS data, see http://www.bis.org/statistics/pp.htm.

C.3Other sources of macroprudential policies

I collect data on macroprudential policies from the IMF’s integrated Macroprudential Policy (iMaPP) Database, originally constructed by Alam et al. (2019). The database provides a dummy-type indicator for tightening and loosening of various macroprudential policy tools, along with a description of each policy action. The database combines information from five existing databases and the IMF’s Macroprudential Policy Survey.8 More information about the database is available at https://www.elibrary-areaer.imf.org/Macroprudential/Pages/Home.aspx. This dataset also contains data on the average LTV ratio. I collapse policies to the annual level in order to match the frequency of the homeownership data.

I construct several indicators for policy actions. The ideal situation would be to have an indicator that captures both the intensity and the timing of any macroprudential policy change. However, since macroprudential policy instruments remain highly country-specific and often expand in scope over time, it is difficult to capture the intensity of a given policy action. I therefore construct policy indicators that capture the timing of policies, following most studies within the field. For instance, Akinci and Olmstead-Rumsey (2018) and Kuttner and Shim (2016) use an indicator that tracks whether a policy action was tightened, loosened or if there was no change. For country i at time t, the indicator s^j_{i,t} denotes whether policy j was tightened, loosened or remained unchanged: s^j_{i,t} = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if policy instrument \textit{j} is tightened}\\ -1, & \text{if policy instrument \textit{j} is loosened}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} I use this indicator to define a net index S^j_{i,k} as: S^j_{i,t} = \sum_{k=0}^t s^j_{i,k} \tag{4} This index represents the net cumulative number of policy actions taken by country i from time k until time t, where k is the earliest year where the homeownership data is available.
