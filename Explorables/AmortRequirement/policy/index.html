Policy summary · Review of Financial Studies, 2026

Mortgage repayment rules change how much households borrow

In 2016 Sweden required new mortgages above half the home’s value to be paid down. Many borrowers responded by borrowing exactly half. Most of them could have afforded to borrow more.

Research by Claes Bäckman (University of Mannheim and SAFE), Patrick Moran (Federal Reserve Board) and Peter van Santen (University of Groningen)

The rule

Sweden made repayment mandatory above 50% loan-to-value

Swedish house prices rose 31% between 2011 and 2015, and credit growth rose from 5% in 2012 to over 8% in 2015. To bring debt down over time, Finansinspektionen, the Swedish financial supervisor, introduced the amortization requirement (amorteringskravet) for all new mortgages from 1 June 2016. Before then, about 60% of new mortgages near 50% loan-to-value were interest-only.

Swedish mortgages are repaid in equal slices of the original loan, so the monthly payment jumps as soon as a loan crosses 50%. The interest rate does not change at the line. Once a borrower has repaid down to 50%, they may stop repaying. At the eight banks in the data this takes a phone call or an online request, with no new credit check. Only one bank charged a fee, of 1,500 SEK (about US$150). Home values can be reassessed only every five years, and only for renovations, so rising market prices do not release a borrower from the requirement.

Checks

Borrowers chose the smaller loans themselves

A pile-up at 50% could in principle come from banks steering customers or adjusting valuations. The authors test each of these channels and find no sign of them. The rule changed which loans borrowers chose.

Interest rates are flat across the line

Average mortgage rates are the same just below and just above 50%, both before and after 2016. Banks did not use pricing to push borrowers toward the threshold.

Refinancers respond like buyers

When existing owners refinance, the bank sets the home value. Their loan-to-value still fell by 2.4 to 2.9 points, close to the 2.2 points for buyers. The response therefore comes from smaller loans.

Valuations show no jump

Assessed home values do not jump at the threshold. Banks value homes mostly with statistical models or the purchase price, and do so before the borrower picks a loan size.

Capital rules have no step at 50%

Neither the banks nor the bankers’ association reported a change in risk weights at the threshold. Swedish mortgages also carry a 25% risk-weight floor.

The effect lasted

The pile-up is about the same size in 2016, 2017 and 2018. Use the year buttons in the first chart to compare.

Earlier years are a sound benchmark

Placebo tests show that years before 2016 predict one another’s loan-to-value distribution well, so they give a reliable picture of borrowing without the rule.

Who responded

Most households that moved to the line could have borrowed more

Swedish banks check that a borrower can afford interest and repayment at a stressed interest rate of about 7% and still cover basic living costs. The authors work out how much monthly income each borrower would have had left over after this test had they borrowed one point more.

Only 13.6% of borrowers at the 50% line would have failed the test. The rest could have borrowed at least another US$67,000 (44% of the sample average) before reaching the limit. Credit constraints therefore cannot explain most of the response.

Why

Households treat repayment as an ongoing cost

A standard economic model of borrowing explains why households short of cash avoid repayment. It does not explain why well-off households do. The authors build such a model, calibrate it to Sweden, and find no pile-up at 50%. In the model, required repayment makes every household worse off, and the loss grows smoothly with the size of the loan, with no step at the line.

They then add two possible reasons for disliking repayment. Each leaves a different mark on the distribution of loans. Pick one to see its pattern.

In the Swedish data, the dip just above 50% is less than 15% of the size of the pile-up. The pattern of an ongoing dislike of repaying fits this best. A one-off hassle can account for a small part at most.

Several kinds of behavior could produce this dislike. Households may aim for a target monthly payment, may not count repayment as saving, or may prefer to leave investments untouched rather than draw on them each month. The study does not tell these explanations apart.

Policy

Repayment schedules are a strong lever on household debt

If households weigh the monthly payment more heavily than the lifetime cost of a loan, then the design of the repayment schedule shapes how much they borrow. The authors use their model to put a size on this.

Lessons for policy design

Repayment rules reach borrowers that affordability caps miss

Near the 50% line, the banks’ affordability test was binding for only about one borrower in seven. The repayment requirement changed borrowing among the rest as well.

Low-initial-payment loans raise debt and lifetime interest

Interest-only loans, longer maturities and other ways to defer repayment raise borrowing well beyond what credit constraints alone predict. The authors expect similar effects in consumer credit with longer maturities or lower initial payments.

Thresholds draw borrowers, and the effect spreads above them

Expect borrowers to gather just below a loan-to-value threshold. In the model that fits the data, borrowers well above the line also borrow less, so the effect on total debt is broader than the spike suggests.

Required repayment adds to household wealth

Because households fund repayment mainly by spending less, required amortization adds close to one-for-one to their net wealth in the form of home equity.

Limits

What the study does not tell you

The study does not say whether the requirement made households better or worse off. Required repayment limits households’ ability to spread spending over time, which the standard model counts as a cost for everyone. If households dislike repaying because of behavioral biases, that may justify regulation. If they dislike it because they prefer to keep money invested elsewhere, it would not. The data cannot separate the two, and the authors caution against reading the results as a verdict on the policy.

The estimates describe the size of new loans at the moment they are taken out. They do not cover households that stayed out of the market, borrowing in later years, house prices, or financial stability.

The study

Data, method and citation

Data. Finansinspektionen’s Mortgage Survey (Bolåneundersökningen), 2011–2018. It records every new mortgage issued by the eight largest Swedish banks on survey days between August and October that the banks learn of only afterwards, with income, debt, loan size, interest rate, repayment and collateral value. The analysis at the 50% line draws on 35,747 loans.

Method. The authors compare the share of new loans in each half-point loan-to-value band after the rule (2016–2018) with the share in the same band before it (2011–2015). The extra loans at the threshold measure the pile-up, and the shortfall just above it measures the hole. A life-cycle model of housing and mortgage choice then tests which explanations reproduce both.
