A true story from Sweden

The Half-a-House Mystery

In 2016, Sweden made a new rule about house loans. Then something strange happened. Lots of families made sure they borrowed exactly half the price of their house. Not a bit more and not a bit less. Why would they do that? Let’s investigate.

Start the case

For curious readers aged 9 and up. Based on research by Claes Bäckman, Patrick Moran and Peter van Santen.

Chapter 1

Buying a house with help from a bank

Houses cost a lot of money. In Sweden, a family home can cost 3 million kronor or more. Kronor are the money people use in Sweden. Hardly any family has that much saved.

So families pay part of the price with their own savings and borrow the rest from a bank.

LoanMoney you borrow and promise to pay back later.

Until the loan is paid back, it is as if the house is shared. Part of it belongs to the family, and part of it belongs to the bank.

Banks don’t lend money for free. Every month the family pays the bank a fee just for borrowing. That fee is called interest.

InterestThe price of borrowing money. It is a bit like paying rent for money.

Chapter 2

Sweden’s new rule

Before 2016, many Swedish families only paid interest each month. They didn’t pay back any of the loan itself.

Sweden’s money watchdog, called Finansinspektionen, worried that families owed too much. So it made a new rule, which started in June 2016.

If the bank owns more than half of the house, the family must pay back a little of the loan every year.
Swedish wordAmortering (say ah-mor-TAY-ring) means paying back a loan bit by bit.

Plan A has the bigger monthly payment, 36 coins instead of 21. It looks much more expensive! But 14 of those coins are not spent. They go into the family’s own part of the house, like coins in a piggy bank. After about 4 years, Plan A’s family owns half the house, just like Plan B’s family.

Chapter 4

The strange pile-up

After the rule began, the researchers looked at tens of thousands of new loans from Sweden’s biggest banks. They sorted the loans by how much of each house the bank owned.

Out of every 100 new loans, how many sat right at half?
7out of 100 after the rule

Each little house is one loan. The red houses are loans where the bank owned right about half.

All the loans, sorted by how much the bank owned
Loans out of every 100, for each half step from 40% to 60%. Tap a bar to see its number.

Before the rule, about 3 in every 100 new loans sat right at half. After the rule, about 7 in 100 did. Families squeezed their loans down to sit exactly on the line.

Here is the puzzle. Paying back a loan doesn’t make money disappear, as you saw with the piggy bank. So why did so many families work so hard to avoid it?

Chapter 5

The suspects

Detectives make a list of suspects and then check the clues. Researchers work the same way. Here are three possible explanations.

Make your guess first. Which suspect do you think it is?

A

They couldn’t afford to pay back

Clue: Banks check that every family can afford its payments, even if interest got much more expensive. Almost 9 out of 10 families at the half-way line could have borrowed more and still passed that check.

Not the main reason
B

The banks pushed them

Clue: Banks charged the same interest just below and just above the line, so they had no reason to push. And the banks decided what each house was worth before the family chose how much to borrow.

Ruled out
C

Paying back feels like losing money

Clue 1: In a survey, about 4 in 10 Swedes said paying back a loan is a cost, like interest.

“It’s a cost” “It’s saving” “I don’t know”

Clue 2: There was no empty gap just above the line. If people only disliked one phone call to the bank, the families just above the line would all have moved down, leaving a gap. The researchers’ computer model shows that no gap is what happens when people dislike every single monthly payment.

Most likely!

Clues checked: 0 of 3

Chapter 6

Case closed: what we learned

Interest is the price of borrowing

Interest goes to the bank and doesn’t come back.

Paying back a loan is a kind of saving

Every krona paid back makes the family’s part of the house bigger.

Monthly payments can fool us

Something can look expensive each month and still be good for you. Something can look cheap each month and cost more in the end.

Rules change how much people borrow

Families who moved to the line borrowed up to about 5% less. In the researchers’ computer model, loans that let families skip paying back made them borrow about one third more.

Was the rule a good idea?

The researchers say we can’t tell yet. Paying back helps families build savings. It also leaves them with less money to spend each month, which can make life harder. Grown-ups are still studying this question.
