Mortgage Design, Repayment Schedules, and Household Borrowing

Claes Bäckman · Patrick Moran · Peter van Santen

Read the paper Cite this paper Replication package All estimates (CSV)

How does the design of debt repayment schedules affect household borrowing? We exploit a Swedish policy reform that eliminated interest-only mortgages for loan-to-value ratios above 50%. We document substantial bunching at the threshold, leading to 5% lower borrowing. Wealthy borrowers drive the results, challenging credit constraints as the primary explanation. A life-cycle model shows that much of the effect comes from households experiencing ongoing flow disutility to amortization payments. Mortgage contracts with low initial payments substantially increase household borrowing and lifetime interest costs.

Setting

The 2016 Swedish amortization requirement

From June 2016, new Swedish mortgages with a loan-to-value ratio above 50% must repay at least 1% of the original loan each year, and those above 70% at least 2%. Below 50%, interest-only remains allowed. Swedish mortgages have linear repayment schedules, so the jump in the monthly payment at the threshold is entirely principal, not interest.

The rule applies to the whole loan, not the part above the threshold. A borrower just above 50% therefore faces a large implicit marginal amortization rate on the last percentage points of LTV. Once the balance falls below the threshold, a phone call to the bank turns amortization off. Seven of the eight large banks charge no fee, there is no new credit check, and no new appraisal.

Data come from Finansinspektionen's Mortgage Survey, 2011–2018, which covers all new mortgages issued by the eight largest banks on sampled days each autumn.

Why the live numbers differ slightly from the paper

The paper bins the confidential microdata by rounding LTV to the nearest 0.5 (so the bin labelled 50 covers 49.75–50.25). The public file behind this page uses bins [a, a+0.5), which cannot be re-rounded. The bin [50, 50.5) mostly holds loans at exactly 50% (70% of them interest-only in 2016), but it also contains some loans just above the threshold. The live estimates are therefore close to, but not identical to, the published ones, and the live page reports no bootstrap standard errors. The published estimate, with its standard error, is always shown alongside. The page publishes shares only, no counts.

Method: the difference-in-bunching estimator

Let nj be the post-reform percent of loans in bin j and n̂j the percent in the same bin before the reform. With threshold T and window limits L and U:

B̂ = Σj=LT (nj − n̂j)  M̂ = Σj>TU (nj − n̂j)

ΔLTV = B̂ / ĝ(T) where ĝ is the average counterfactual density over [L, T]

eα = (ΔLTV / T) / (α* − α0), α* = α0 + Δα + Δα · T / ΔLTV

The identifying assumption is that, absent the reform, the share of loans in each bin would have equalled its pre-reform share. This allows for round-number spikes at 50 that are stable over time, which a smooth polynomial counterfactual would attribute to the policy. M is negative when mass is missing above the threshold. The estimation sample is LTV 20–65 for the 50% threshold and 55–80 for the 70% threshold. At 50, α0 = 0 and Δα = 0.01. At 70, α0 = 0.01 and Δα = 0.01.

Supply-side checks

Mortgage rates show no discontinuity at the threshold, before or after the reform. The share of interest-only loans above 50% collapses after the reform, which is the policy itself.

Calibration and identification

Move the disutility parameter and read off the excess mass and missing mass the model implies. A flow disutility of Δk = 0.08 reproduces the empirical excess mass (model B = 7.53, data B = 7.47) with almost no hole. A one-off cost generates a large hole relative to the pile, which the data do not show.

Implications

Why a kink matters beyond Sweden

Financial innovation and household debt

Start from a model economy with only fully amortizing mortgages and introduce interest-only loans. Mortgage debt rises by 33.4% when households have flow disutility, and by 9.1% without it. A notch would affect only borrowers near a threshold. A kink shifts everyone who amortizes, which is why the amplification is large.

Forced repayment and wealth accumulation

Bernstein and Koudijs (2024) find that each euro of mandatory amortization raises net wealth by almost a euro, even for households with liquid savings. Re-running their instrumental-variables design on simulated data, only the model with flow disutility matches both facts.
