YoHo — Young Homeownership

A Marie Skłodowska-Curie Action project on financial barriers to homeownership among young households.

About the project

YoHo (Young Homeownership) is funded by a two-year Marie Skłodowska-Curie Action grant I obtained from the European Union in 2023. The project examines the financial barriers that prevent young households from becoming homeowners, and studies how mortgage-market design, macroprudential policy, and housing supply interact with household credit constraints over the life cycle.

The project is hosted at the Leibniz Institute for Financial Research SAFE in Frankfurt, with data and collaborations extending across the Nordic countries and the EU.

Projects

Macroprudential Policies and Homeownership Rates

PDF · Digital edition · SAFE Blog post

Abstract

Macroprudential policies are a key policy tool for financial regulators, but concerns persist that these policies restrict access to homeownership. I examine this concern using cross-country data on homeownership for 28 countries. I find little evidence that macroprudential policies reduce homeownership rates in aggregate or for select groups such as low-income households. The estimates are precise enough to rule out large negative effects of macroprudential policies on homeownership rates. The null effects are consistent with models where credit shocks primarily affect prices rather than quantities. My results alleviate concerns that macroprudential policies systematically exclude certain households from ownership, but also indicate that relaxing such policies is unlikely to increase access to homeownership.

Mortgage Constraints: Household Responses, Market Adjustment, and Financial Stability

with Ragnar E. Juelsrud and Ella Getz Wold

Revise and resubmit, Journal of Economic Literature

PDF · Digital edition · Interactive version

Abstract

Mortgage constraints are widely used to reduce household leverage, but lower credit volumes alone say little about who bears the cost or whether financial vulnerability falls. We decompose the effect of a mortgage constraint tightening into three components: the direct effect on households, the equilibrium incidence through house prices, rents and credit supply and, finally, the corrective benefit. We use this framework to synthesize household-level, housing-market, lender, cross-country and credit-crisis evidence. The direct effect falls on young and low-wealth households, largely through fewer mortgage originations. Aggregate credit responses are several times larger than mechanically aggregated micro responses, consistent with general equilibrium amplification, although settings and outcomes differ across studies. Quantitative evidence on the corrective benefit is scarce, and few of the 154 tightening episodes we examine occur when credit and house-price growth is high.

Housing Capital Gains across the Income Distribution

with Natalia Khorunzhina and Walter D'Lima

PDF · Interactive explainer · Slides · Portfolio decomposition calculator

Abstract

We show that high-income buyers earn higher capital gains on housing using detailed transaction data from Denmark. Geographic location statistically accounts for nearly all the difference, with little role for aggregate market timing, property type, or other buyer characteristics. This finding is consistent with income-based sorting, whereby higher-income households systematically sort into locations with persistently higher price growth. We test whether credit conditions shape access to locations with higher house-price growth and find no detectable change in buyer composition by income rank around major credit expansions and contractions.

Workshop

YoHo funded the first Annual Workshop in Real Estate Finance, held at the Frankfurt School of Finance & Management on 24–25 August 2026. I organized the workshop together with Cristian Badarinza (Frankfurt School). The theme was Housing Market Frictions and Access to Homeownership. Sessions covered housing affordability, housing supply and regulation, real estate intermediaries, natural disasters and property insurance, and the link between housing markets and monetary policy. Andreas Fuster (EPFL and Swiss Finance Institute) and Timothy McQuade (UC Berkeley Haas) gave the keynote lectures. Participants came from universities such as UC Berkeley, Columbia, Northwestern, Toronto and Peking University, and from the European Central Bank, the Bank of Italy and the Bank of Canada.
