# Feedback on "Electricity Prices and Household Electricity Use in Swedish Municipalities, 2015–2023"

This report assesses thesis.md against the six criteria in rubric.md. Section, table and figure numbers refer to the thesis. For each criterion the report gives the evidence and the one change that would most improve the thesis on that criterion. Where the evidence matches a rubric descriptor, the report names it, but the grade remains the examiner's decision.

## Summary

Three problems run through several criteria. First, the headline elasticity is misread from the thesis's own table. Table 2, column 2 reports −0.045, but the abstract and Sections 1, 5, 6 and 7 all report −0.45, ten times larger. Every comparison with the literature and the policy calculation rests on the wrong number. Second, the thesis treats the association between bidding-area spot prices and municipal electricity use as a causal effect without saying where the price variation comes from or what else varies with it. Third, one reference, Holmqvist and Saarinen (2019), could not be verified, and it supplies the only Sweden-specific benchmark. On the rubric's pass rule (at least *sufficient* on every criterion), criteria 4 and 5 currently fall short, and criteria 2 and 6 depend on whether the unverified reference can be produced.

## 1. Research question and motivation

**Evidence.** Section 1 states the question clearly: "How do Swedish households change their electricity use when the price of electricity changes?" The first paragraph of Section 1 links it to the 2021–2022 price spike and to Sweden's energy-efficiency goals, and the third paragraph identifies the price differences between bidding areas as the variation that makes the question answerable. These are strengths.

The question is broader than what the data can answer. It asks about households, but the data are municipal aggregates (Section 3). It asks how households "change" their use, but the main specification (Section 4, Table 2 column 2) compares municipalities in different bidding areas in the same year rather than following the same municipalities over time. Only column 3 uses changes within municipalities. The question the thesis actually answers is how municipal per-capita household use co-moves with the annual spot price of the municipality's bidding area. The motivation says the answer matters "for both energy policy and climate policy" but does not say which decision it would inform or for whom. The contribution in the fourth paragraph of Section 1 is stated in general terms ("new estimates for Sweden using recent data that cover the energy crisis"). The same paragraph claims the estimate "is larger than many earlier estimates" without citing any, and Section 6 contradicts this by calling the estimate close to Reiss and White (2005) and smaller than Holmqvist and Saarinen (2019). The evidence fits the *sufficient* descriptor (the question is broad and the thesis answers a somewhat different question), although the motivation itself is at the *good* level.

**Most important change.** Restate the research question at the level of the data and the variation actually used, namely municipal per-capita household use and the divergence in bidding-area prices after 2020, and state the specific contribution relative to the closest Swedish estimate.

## 2. Literature and hypotheses

**Evidence.** Section 2 covers relevant studies: household demand elasticities (Reiss and White 2005), price perception under nonlinear tariffs (Ito 2014), non-price interventions (Allcott 2011), short-run versus long-run elasticities (Labandeira, Labeaga and López-Otero 2017) and a Nordic estimate (Holmqvist and Saarinen 2019). The four studies that could be checked are summarised accurately (see criterion 6).

The review summarises the studies one at a time, one paragraph each. It does not compare them, explain why their estimates differ, or identify a gap that this thesis fills. Several of the reviewed findings bear directly on the thesis design but are never used. Ito (2014) implies that households respond to the price on their bill, which is not the wholesale spot price used in Section 3. Labandeira et al. (2017) imply that annual data from a nine-year panel mostly capture short-run responses, which matters for interpreting the estimate and for the tax calculation in Section 6. Allcott (2011) implies that non-price factors such as information and appeals to save electricity can shift use, and such factors were prominent during the 2022 crisis in the same regions where prices rose.

H1 is tested in Section 5. H2, that the price response is larger where detached houses are more common, is never tested. Table 2 contains no interaction between price and the detached-house share. The second paragraph of Section 5 interprets the positive coefficient on the detached-house share as "consistent with detached houses being heated with electricity", but that coefficient concerns the level of use, not the size of the price response that H2 predicts. The evidence fits the *sufficient* descriptor (studies summarised one by one, hypotheses stated). If Holmqvist and Saarinen (2019) cannot be verified, the criterion falls to *insufficient* under the rubric's condition that "sources cannot be verified".

**Most important change.** Test H2 by adding an interaction between log price and the detached-house share to the regressions in Table 2.

## 3. Data and method

**Evidence.** Section 3 names a source for each variable (Statistics Sweden's municipal and regional energy statistics, Nord Pool, and Statistics Sweden for income and housing), gives units, and explains how the per-capita measure is built. Table 1 and Figure 1 are informative, and Figure 1 shows clearly that prices barely differed across bidding areas before 2020. Section 4 gives the estimating equation and describes the three specifications.

The documentation has gaps. Section 3 says the panel has 2,610 observations and that municipalities with missing values are dropped, yet Table 1 reports N = 2,610 for every variable and Table 2 uses 2,358. The difference of 252 observations equals 28 municipalities times nine years, which suggests that 28 municipalities are missing from every regression. The thesis does not say which observations were dropped or why. Table 1 describes the full panel rather than the estimation sample. The thesis does not say whether prices are nominal or deflated. The price is the wholesale spot price, while households pay a retail price that adds energy tax, VAT and network charges, and households on fixed-price contracts did not face the spot price at all. This measurement problem is not mentioned. Winsorising the price at the 1st and 99th percentiles has no clear purpose for a variable that takes only 36 distinct values (four areas times nine years).

Section 4 states no identifying assumption and discusses no threat to interpretation. Three threats matter most. The first is omitted variables. In column 2, with year fixed effects, the price coefficient comes from comparing northern and southern municipalities in the same year. Northern municipalities are colder and pay lower prices, so climate alone could produce a negative association between price and use. In column 3 the coefficient comes from the post-2020 divergence between SE3/SE4 and SE1/SE2 (Figure 1), so anything else that changed differently in southern Sweden in 2021–2023 is confounded with price. Candidates include weather, the government's electricity-price compensation to households, public energy-saving campaigns and heat-pump adoption. The second threat concerns inference. The price varies only at the bidding-area-by-year level, yet Section 4 uses heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors that treat 2,358 observations as independent, which overstates precision. Clustering by bidding area leaves only four clusters and would itself need a small-cluster method such as the wild cluster bootstrap. The third threat is simultaneity. Household demand in a bidding area helps set that area's spot price, and cold years raise both. The evidence fits the *sufficient* descriptor (documentation has gaps and the main threats are not discussed). The unexplained loss of 28 municipalities brings the thesis close to the *insufficient* condition that the sample cannot be reconstructed.

**Most important change.** Add an identification discussion to Section 4 that states where the price variation comes from in each column and addresses the main omitted variable, climate, by controlling for heating degree days by municipality and year.

## 4. Results and analysis

**Evidence.** Table 2 is clearly laid out, with standard errors, fixed-effect indicators, observation counts and R². The text identifies the sign of each coefficient correctly.

The text misreports the main result. Table 2, column 2 reports a price coefficient of −0.045 (standard error 0.012). The first paragraph of Section 5 reads this as −0.45 and states that "a 10 percent increase in the electricity price reduces household electricity use by 4.5 percent". The table implies 0.45 percent. The same −0.45 appears in the abstract and in Sections 1, 6 and 7. A check against Figure 1 shows why the larger number is implausible. The SE4 price rose from 28 to 152 öre/kWh between 2020 and 2022, a log change of about 1.69. An elasticity of −0.45 would imply that household use in SE4 roughly halved over two years, whereas −0.045 implies a fall of about 7 percent.

The third paragraph of Section 5 states that the column 3 estimate "remains significant at the 1 percent level". Table 2 marks it with two stars, and −0.031 with a standard error of 0.015 gives a t-statistic of about 2.1, which is significant at the 5 percent level only. The same paragraph concludes that the result "is robust to unobserved differences between municipalities". The estimate falls by about a third from column 2 and by about 70 percent from column 1, and income and the detached-house share lose significance once municipality fixed effects are added. A coefficient that moves this much as controls are added shows that the specification matters, not that the result is robust. The text does not discuss why the estimate falls from −0.112 to −0.045 between columns 1 and 2.

Interpretation of magnitudes is thin. The control coefficients are read only by sign. Their size is informative. In column 2, a one-standard-deviation higher detached-house share (17.9 percentage points, Table 1) is associated with about 17 percent higher use, while moving from the lowest to the highest price in the sample (14 to 152 öre/kWh) is associated with about 10 percent lower use. The income interpretation in the second paragraph of Section 5 attributes municipal differences to household appliance ownership and home size, which municipal data cannot show. Causal language ("higher prices cause households to save electricity" in the abstract, with the same claim in Sections 6 and 7) goes beyond what OLS on bidding-area prices can show without the identification argument discussed under criterion 3. The evidence fits the *insufficient* descriptor (results are misreported and the text contradicts the table).

**Most important change.** Correct the main elasticity from −0.45 to −0.045 in the abstract and Sections 1, 5, 6 and 7, and interpret it as a 0.45 percent fall in use for a 10 percent price increase.

## 5. Discussion, conclusions and sustainability

**Evidence.** Section 6 compares the result with earlier estimates, offers an explanation for the differences, draws a sustainability implication and lists limitations. Section 7 answers the research question. The structure of the discussion is appropriate.

The conclusions do not follow from the results in Table 2. Because the estimate is −0.045, the first paragraph of Section 6 inverts the comparison with the literature. The estimate is about one tenth of the −0.4 in Reiss and White (2005), not close to it. The explanation offered for the gap to Holmqvist and Saarinen (2019), that households had "already made the easy savings", is not tested and would have to account for a gap of an order of magnitude rather than a modest one. The tax calculation in the second paragraph of Section 6, a 9 percent fall in use for a 20 percent price rise, becomes about 0.9 percent with the column 2 estimate and about 0.6 percent with column 3.

The sustainability implication is overstated even apart from the number. The thesis does not link electricity savings to emissions. Swedish electricity generation is largely fossil-free, and the power sector falls under the EU Emissions Trading System, so lower household electricity use does not translate directly into lower emissions. The thesis does not mention that Swedish household electricity is already subject to an energy tax, and it does not discuss the distributional effect of a further tax on households in southern Sweden, whose bills Section 1 says had already doubled. An elasticity with respect to the spot price also need not carry over to a tax, because a tax changes the retail price, of which the spot price is only one component.

The limitations paragraph in Section 6 is generic. It names aggregation, the short period and the lack of household data, but not the specific threats listed under criterion 3 (climate, the post-2020 confounders, standard errors, spot versus retail price), and it does not say how any limitation would change the conclusions. Section 7 repeats the causal claim and the tax recommendation. The evidence fits the *insufficient* descriptor (conclusions do not follow from the results). Once the number is corrected, the remaining problems would match *sufficient* (generic limitations and overstated implications).

**Most important change.** Recalibrate the policy implication in Sections 6 and 7 to the corrected estimate, which means recomputing the tax effect and withdrawing the claim that an electricity tax would be an effective climate policy unless the thesis shows how saved electricity translates into lower emissions.

## 6. Structure, language and references

**Evidence.** The structure is clear. The roadmap at the end of Section 1 matches Sections 2 to 7, each section does what its heading says, and the language is plain and readable. All five references follow a consistent author-date format with journal, volume, issue and pages.

Numbers are inconsistent across the text. The elasticity is −0.45 in the abstract and Sections 1, 5, 6 and 7, but −0.045 in Table 2. The sample is 2,610 observations in Section 3 and Table 1, but 2,358 in Table 2. The abstract, Section 3 and Section 7 refer to "all 290 Swedish municipalities", but 2,358 observations over nine years correspond to 262 municipalities. Section 3 refers to Figure 2, which is missing, and Figure 1 is presented as a table. The abstract claims robustness to municipality fixed effects and Section 5 claims 1 percent significance for column 3, while Table 2 shows two stars. The equation in Section 4 has no municipality or year subscripts, so the reader cannot tell which variables vary at which level.

The references were checked against the reviewer's own knowledge, without a web search. Allcott (2011), Ito (2014), Labandeira, Labeaga and López-Otero (2017) and Reiss and White (2005) match published articles, and Section 2 represents their findings accurately. Holmqvist and Saarinen (2019), "Residential Electricity Demand in the Nordic Countries", *Energy Economics* 81: 112–25, could not be verified and has no DOI. It matters because it is the only Sweden-specific source, it supplies the −0.6 benchmark used in Section 6, and it supports the claim in Section 2 that Sweden's elasticity is "among the highest in Europe". The evidence fits the *sufficient* descriptor on structure and numbers. If the Holmqvist and Saarinen reference cannot be verified, the criterion falls to *insufficient* (references unverifiable).

**Most important change.** Verify Holmqvist and Saarinen (2019) by adding its DOI, or remove it together with the −0.6 benchmark and the "among the highest in Europe" claim that depend on it.
