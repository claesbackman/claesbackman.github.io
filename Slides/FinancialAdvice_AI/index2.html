One Advisor for the Whole World?

Cross-country evidence on financial advice from large language models

Claes Bäckman

Leibniz Institute for Financial Research SAFE

Who is told to hold more stock?

The advice varies little depending on the country

The model grid

Households everywhere now ask the same few models for financial advice

  • LLMs are becoming financial advisors. Over one third of individuals across OECD countries reported using AI tools (OECD 2026). 51% of US consumers reported using AI to obtain financial information or advice (J.D. Power 2025)
  • LLM advice comes from a handful of models. The models are trained overwhelmingly on U.S.-English text, and the same ones answer in every language.
  • Advice markets are national. They reflect local norms, pension systems and regulation.
  • This paper. Does LLM advice differ across countries, and does it adapt to local circumstances?

Prior work varies the investor and holds the country fixed

  • Choukhmane et al. (2026). U.S. households’ own prompts against a life-cycle model: moderately risk-averse advice in round numbers.
  • Ross and Lo (2026). Synthetic U.S. clients, stated risk tolerance carries most of the weight.
  • Foltyn and Olsson (2026). Gender gaps in financial advice in English are under two points.
  • Human and robo-advisors. Advisor fixed effects dominate client traits (Foerster et al. 2017), and robo-advice improves diversification (D’Acunto et al. 2019; Rossi and Utkus 2024).

This paper

  • Does the advice differ across countries?
    → The portfolio answer barely does: small gaps, all in one direction.
  • How do the models explain their advice?
    → They know the country, but only use it when required: local words in the explanations, country differences on the mortgage.
  • Does the advice track country fundamentals?
    → Uniform advice is defensible, but the balance sheet should matter. Advice does not track measured risk aversion or financial literacy
  • What rule produces the advice?
    → A retail risk questionnaire, or something akin to “popular financial advice” (Canner et al. 1997; Choi 2022).

Experimental design

Each model is asked three questions

  • Portfolio. How to split the local equivalent of $50,000 between a broad stock-market index fund and government bonds over five years. Ages 30, 40 and 55.
  • Mortgage. Whether to invest savings in an index fund or prepay a 4% mortgage with twenty years left. Age 40.
  • Inflation. How to protect $50,000 earning 1% in a savings account against 3% inflation: the fraction to move and the primary instrument. Age 40.

Empirical specification

y_{imc} = \alpha_m + \gamma_c + \varepsilon_{imc}

  • Within model. y_{imc} is the equity share in repetition i by model m for country c. The model fixed effects \alpha_m make each \gamma_c a within-model contrast with the U.S.
  • Thirteen clusters. Standard errors cluster on 13 models
  • Multiple testing. The twenty country contrasts form one Holm family, so the chance of even one false rejection among them is at most 5 percent.
  • Pre-registered. Filed at OSF on 2 July 2026 and collected on 3–4 July. Primary cell: the portfolio question, Version B, age 40.

Should advice differ across countries?

A way to think about the share across countries

For the investor with stake L, write the optimal share as: \theta^{*}_c = \min\left\{1,\; \overbrace{{\color{#C4452A}\frac{\mu_c - r_c}{\gamma_c\,\sigma_c^{2}}}}^{\text{Merton (1969)}} \times \underbrace{\left(1+\frac{B_c}{L}\right)}_{\text{Adjustment for other wealth}}\right\}

  • Risk aversion \gamma_c. is not stated in the prompt, but could be imputed by the model.
  • The equity premium \mu_c - r_c is the same in every currency under uncovered interest parity.
  • Volatility \sigma_c may or may not differ by country depending on currency hedging (Campbell and Viceira 2002).
  • Adjustment for other wealth. Counting the pension and human capital as safe wealth B_c beside the stake raises the share in stocks (Bodie et al. 1992; Cocco et al. 2005)

Answer depends whether the advice should take currency hedging into account

The country effects are only weakly related to national risk preferences and literacy

Risk taking from Falk et al. (2018). Literacy from Klapper et al. (2015).

What rule produces the advice?

What happens if you change the investor?

\theta^{*}_c = \min\left\{1,\; \overbrace{\frac{\mu_c - r_c}{\textcolor{#C4452A}{\gamma_c}\,\sigma_c^{2}}}^{\text{Merton (1969)}} \times \underbrace{\left(1+\frac{\textcolor{#C4452A}{B^{s}_c} + \textcolor{#C4452A}{B^{r}_c}}{L}\right)}_{\text{total wealth over the stake}} - \underbrace{\frac{\textcolor{#C4452A}{B^{r}_c}}{L}}_{\text{stocks held outside}}\right\}

  • Risk tolerance moves \gamma. “I am comfortable with substantial fluctuations…” against “I am uncomfortable with large fluctuations…”.
  • Pension generosity moves B^{s}. “A substantial pension…, projected to replace most of my pre-retirement income” against “only a small pension”.
  • The horizon is not in the formula. “Over a five-year horizon” becomes one year or twenty.

One rule answering every country makes its errors common and hard to see

  • Errors are common, not idiosyncratic. A handful of models answer every country, so a mistake in one rule reaches every household that asks it. Human advice already clusters by advisor (Foerster et al. 2017).
  • The neglected margin is the one suitability rules target. Those rules oblige a human advisor to weigh the client’s balance sheet. The models weigh it only as a hazard.
  • The uniformity is hard to see. The explanation adapts to the country, and the number does not.
  • Outside the perimeter. The same service, supplied by humans, is regulated country by country.

Conclusions

  • Small, one-sided country effects. Country averages span 12 points and model averages 34, and every gap that survives correction is negative.
  • The models know the country. Explanations fill with local context while the share barely moves, but on the mortgage question the country explains more of the variance than the model.
  • Uniformity is defensible, the balance sheet is not. A hedged world fund rationalizes one number everywhere, and theory and the advice part on the balance sheet.
  • A questionnaire, not a balance-sheet rule. A stated horizon moves the advice 40 points, a stated pension about 1, and stated safety raises it by at most 5.

Each of a handful of models answers households in every language, so an error in its rule reaches everyone who asks it.

The design observes advice, not the computation behind it

  • No model internals. The shared-representation reading is an inference from behaviour, consistent with interpretability work, not a measurement.
  • A static benchmark. Closed form and one period. The one life-cycle treatment of LLM advice reaches the same corner (Choukhmane et al. 2026).
  • A fast-moving panel. The main collection is a two-day snapshot. Eleven weeks later DeepSeek V4 Flash gave 8.5 points more equity under an unchanged identifier, so replications need the collection date.
