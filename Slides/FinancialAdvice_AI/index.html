One Advisor for the Whole World?

Cross-country evidence on financial advice from large language models

Claes Bäckman

Leibniz Institute for Financial Research SAFE

Two investors, one question. Who is told to hold more stock?

The hook. Emily and Lena are the running example for the whole talk. They are fictional, and every number attached to them is the United States or the Germany cell of the real collection.

Why it matters, in one sentence: the share of U.S. adults who use AI tools for financial advice roughly doubled between 2024 and 2025 (Financial Health Network 2025), and a handful of models answer them in every language. Every prompt also carries the same English instructions asking for a JSON answer: a fraction for the stock fund and an explanation of at most 200 words.

Read the question out. Then the bet: “Show of hands. Who thinks Lena is told to hold less stock than Emily? More? About the same?” Most rooms say less. If they hesitate, give them the textbook reason: a euro investor who buys a world index fund carries exchange-rate risk that a dollar investor does not, so a careful advisor might give Lena less. That reason comes back on the benchmark slide, where the euro turns out to add almost none.

Hold the silence for a second, then advance.

Prior work varies the investor and holds the country fixed

  • Choukhmane, de Silva, Lin and Akuzawa (2026). U.S. households’ own prompts against a life-cycle model: moderately risk-averse advice in round numbers.
  • Ross and Lo (2026). Synthetic U.S. clients and one vendor’s models: stated risk tolerance carries most of the weight.
  • Foltyn and Olsson (2026). Similar design to this paper, shows gender gaps under two points.

Thirty seconds. The first two are the closest papers, and both hold the investor’s country fixed by construction. Ross and Lo call the dominance of stated risk tolerance “heuristic collapse” and reach the same conclusion about the advice rule from the other direction, which strengthens both readings.

Foltyn and Olsson is the framework this experiment adapts, replacing gender with country. Keep their under-two-points gender gap in mind as a yardstick. What this paper adds beyond the treatment: a country-calibrated benchmark, and the explanation text kept and coded.

For robo-advice people: D’Acunto, Prabhala and Rossi (2019) and Rossi and Utkus (2024) find first-generation robo-advisors improving diversification. LLMs differ in being general-purpose and deployed in every language at once. Human advisors already give one-size-fits-all advice (Foerster et al. 2017).

The design in one slide. Fifteen countries are asked in the local language and six in English (U.S., UK, Canada, Australia, India, South Africa), so B-cross exists for the fifteen. The amount is the round local-currency equivalent of $50,000 in native number format.

How the inference works · Robustness

Point at the top: Brazil, Italy, Poland, France, Spain, the Netherlands, Mexico. Southern and central Europe plus Latin America. Every contrast that survives is negative, and the U.S. sits near the top of the band.

The yardstick for the rest of the talk: country effects are 3 to 11 points. That is small beside the 34-point model spread and beside what the stated investor does later, but larger than the under-two-points gender gaps of Foltyn and Olsson.

Whether these gaps are errors is the benchmark slide’s question. Brazil’s benchmark is low, so its gap may be a move toward theory. Germany: −4.0 points, interval −7.7 to −0.4, so it rejects on its own (p = 0.03) but not after Holm (p = 0.34), one of the hollow rings.

Inference: model fixed effects and clustering by model. The joint test of equal advice in all 21 countries gives p = 0.006. With 13 clusters every p-value is a wild cluster bootstrap with Webb weights. The minimum detectable single contrast is about four points, so the nulls rule out large gaps, not gaps of a point or two. If asked about the rank of the covariance matrix: the joint test is the bootstrap Wald statistic. The asymptotic cluster-robust test can impose only 12 of the 20 restrictions with 13 clusters, and it also rejects, F(12, 12) = 34.26.

Country-by-country decomposition · The dollar-denominated stake

Two numbers, each about a point. Asking Lena’s question in German rather than English lowers the share by 1.3 points: significant, and an order of magnitude below the largest country effects. Naming the bAV, an MSCI World ETF and Bunds moves it by −0.7, not significant at 5 percent.

Why a small language effect is plausible: interpretability work finds that multilingual models solve tasks in a shared concept space that sits closer to English than to any other language (Wendler et al. 2024). It was not guaranteed: the same model often asserts different facts in different languages.

The United Kingdom is the pre-registered test of naming a country without changing the language: +0.7 points, interval −0.8 to +2.2, inside the ±5 margin registered for that test alone.

The country reference also changes the currency and the numeral of the amount, so “the country” here is the country, its currency and its number format together. The dollar-stake cell rules the numeral out for Germany, Japan and Sweden.

Bridge: so the name of the country moves the number a little. Does the model know anything about the country?

The explanations localize, but the number barely moves

I use a separate model, shown only the explanation text, to code whether it names local instruments, taxes or pensions.

How the text is coded

Start on Version B. Point at the two coloured rows: the U.S. at 18 percent and Germany at 63. Outside the U.S. the models reach for local instruments, taxes and pensions in half to nearly all of their explanations. So they know where the investor lives. Both panels share one 0 to 100 percent scale: the words range across the whole axis, the numbers sit in a narrow band.

The move: flip the switch to Version C, where the prompt names each country’s own pension, bonds and index fund. The hatched bars jump toward 100 percent, a 23-point rise, and the dots barely move, −0.7 points pooled. Dashed rings mark where each dot was. Brazil moves most, about 3 points, and the pooled effect is not significant at 5 percent.

The same country regression that finds a few points on the equity share finds up to 78 points on whether local context is named, so the design can see country variation where the answers contain it.

Objection, “the coder just finds the words you put in the prompt”: that is why Version B, which names no instruments, is the main test, and 47 to 96 percent there is unprompted. Under Version C the U.S. rate rises from 18 to 84 percent, so the coder does count U.S. instruments as local context.

If the switch fails, the caption carries the result.

The teal marks are theory’s direction only. With constant relative risk aversion and independent returns the horizon is irrelevant. A lifecycle planner who counts pension wealth treats a generous pension as a bond and holds more stock.

Why English: the statements are added to the English prompt (Version B in the six English-speaking countries, B-cross elsewhere), so no translation stands between the sentence and the model.

Objection, “a one-year horizon is a spending date, and any planner holds bonds for that”: the twenty-year cell alone sits about 20 points above the five-year prompt with no added sentence, so the response is not only year-one liquidity.

Ross and Lo find the same dominance of risk tolerance with one vendor. New here: 13 models, 21 countries, and the contrast with a stated pension.

Measured: the stated-investor effects of the previous slide, in percentage points. Struck through and marked ✗: a prediction the measured effect contradicts. The comparison follows the popular-advice reading of Canner, Mankiw and Weil (1997) and Choi (2022).

Walk the columns left to right. The Merton planner gets risk tolerance and the pension right, because on the stake alone a pension does not enter, but it predicts no horizon effect, and the data show forty points. The lifecycle planner is contradicted twice. The questionnaire asks the two questions the models respond to and does not ask about the balance sheet.

The questionnaire column is the one reading consistent with all three. It is a reading, and the paper says “fits”, not “proves”. The levels also fit a Merton planner with relative risk aversion between 2 and 5, so the levels alone cannot discriminate. The comparative statics can.

Objection, “mean reversion rationalizes the horizon effect” (Campbell and Viceira 2002): yes, but a planner sophisticated enough to price return dynamics into forty points should not ignore pension wealth.

Bridge: the pension null is not blindness, though. The next slide shows the balance sheet does register, on one side.

Stated risks cut the stock share sharply, stated safety barely raises it

Exploratory probes collected in August 2026, after the pre-registered results were examined.

Read the pairs top to bottom. A pension invested in stocks: −17 points, the right sign. A guaranteed inflation-linked annuity, which should crowd stock in: +4.8, barely significant. The stake is all my liquid wealth: −23. The stake is a third of my liquid wealth with twice as much in safe savings: +2.5, when the benchmark is already at its 100 percent corner. The hedge: nothing either way, for a statement that should matter most where currencies are volatile.

What to say: the models de-risk on stated exposure and let stated safety raise the share very little. The paper calls this one-sided prudence rather than blindness. The neglected side is the margin that suitability rules oblige a human advisor to weigh.

Objection, “all my liquid wealth means no emergency buffer, and cutting stock is standard advice”: agreed, and the benchmark has the same sign. The asymmetry is on the safe side, where a large cushion barely moves the number.

The wealth statements were asked in the U.S. prompt only, and a third wealth statement, half of liquid wealth, gives +1.4. Everything here is exploratory and post hoc, tested two-sided against the benchmark’s sign.

The portfolio answer follows the model and a questionnaire far more than the country

  • Small, one-sided country effects. Country averages span 12 points, model averages 34, and a hedged world fund rationalizes one number everywhere.
  • Local words, a nearly unchanged number. Local context in 47 to 96% of non-U.S. explanations, and naming the local instruments moves the share by under a point.
  • Localization where local prices decide. On the mortgage question the country explains more of the variance than the model.
  • A questionnaire, not a balance-sheet rule. A stated horizon moves the advice 40 points, a stated pension about 1, and stated safety barely registers.

Each of a handful of models answers households in every language, so an error in its rule reaches everyone who asks it.

Four claims, one breath each. The numbers behind them if someone wants them: seven significant contrasts, all negative, 3 to 11 points, and hedged theory at 52 percent everywhere. Version C raises local references by 23 points and moves the share by −0.7. On the mortgage question the country explains 27 percent of the variance and the model 15. Stated tolerance moves the advice 46 points, stated risks cut it by up to 23, stated safety raises it by at most 5.

Then the market-structure sentence: advice used to be a nationally segmented service, walled in by regulation, trust and language. The same handful of models now answer in every language at negligible marginal cost, outside the regulatory perimeter that bound human advisors. The error is common within a model, across all the households that ask it.

Limits, if asked: no model internals, so the shared-representation reading is an inference from behaviour. The benchmark is static and closed form. The panel moves fast, though the September re-collection keeps the structure.

Thirteen models are thirteen clusters

Estimation

The advice y_{imc} of repetition i by model m for country c is regressed on model fixed effects and country effects relative to the U.S.

Standard errors cluster on the model. With 13 clusters every p-value is a wild cluster bootstrap with Webb six-point weights and 9,999 replications.

The twenty country contrasts are one Holm family. The minimum detectable single contrast is about 4 points.

Collection

Temperature 0.7, no system prompt, at most 4,000 tokens, truncated answers excluded.

A JSON-first parser recovered a number from 99.9% of answers. There were no refusals in 267,150 answers.

The joint test of equal advice in all 21 countries gives F(20, 12) = 20.56, p = 0.006.

Back to the countries · Back to the design

Why the model is the unit: repetitions within a model are not independent, so the effective sample is the thirteen models, not the quarter-million answers. Asymptotic CR1 with G = 13 over-rejects badly, which is why the bootstrap is the inference of record.

The pilot of about 6,800 queries passed three pre-specified gates before filing: parse rate at least 95 percent per model, refusal ceilings, and between-model dispersion of the country contrast no larger than 0.06.

The country effects are only weakly related to national risk preferences and literacy

Back to the talk

A country one standard deviation more risk-tolerant gets under three points more equity, and the widest preference contrast in the panel, Japan to South Africa, predicts under four. South Africa, by far the most risk-tolerant, gets slightly less than the U.S.

This is descriptive: the prompt describes one investor, not a country mean, and imputing national risk-taking to her is an ecological inference an advisor need not make. Leave-one-out slopes stay positive and small. The two-stage bootstrap intervals exclude zero, and the wild bootstrap over countries does not.

The benchmark: the Merton share for the stake, and a corner once the balance sheet counts

Benchmark S: the stake alone

\theta^{*}_c = \dfrac{\mu_c - r_c}{\gamma\,\sigma_c^{2}} at \gamma = 3. The equity premium is common across currencies by uncovered interest parity, so only \sigma_c differs.

Benchmark I: the whole balance sheet

Pension wealth and human capital enter as safe outside wealth B, scaling the demand of the liquid stake L to \theta^{*}(1 + B/L).

For a $50,000 stake that is at the 100% corner in every country, far above any answer in the data.

Inverting the Merton rule, the advice implies relative risk aversion near 2 for stated-tolerant, 5 for stated-averse and 3 for no statement.
