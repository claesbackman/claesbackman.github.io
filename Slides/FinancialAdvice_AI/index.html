Households everywhere now ask the same few models for financial advice

  • LLMs are becoming financial advisors. Over one third of individuals across OECD countries reported using AI tools (OECD 2026). 51% of US consumers reported using AI to obtain financial information or advice (J.D. Power 2025)
  • LLM advice comes from a handful of models. The models are trained overwhelmingly on U.S.-English text.
  • Advice markets are national. They reflect local norms, pension systems and regulation.
  • This paper. Does LLM advice differ across countries, and does it adapt to local circumstances?

Prior work varies the investor

  • Choukhmane et al. (2026). U.S. households’ own prompts against a life-cycle model: moderately risk-averse advice in round numbers.
  • Ross and Lo (2026). Synthetic U.S. clients, stated risk tolerance carries most of the weight.
  • Foltyn and Olsson (2026). Gender gaps in financial advice in English are under two points.
  • Human and robo-advisors. Advisor fixed effects dominate client traits (Foerster et al. 2017), and robo-advice improves diversification (D’Acunto et al. 2019; Rossi and Utkus 2024).

One minute. The first two are the closest papers, and both hold the investor’s country fixed by construction. Ross and Lo call the dominance of stated risk tolerance “heuristic collapse” and reach the same conclusion about the advice rule from the other direction, which strengthens both readings.

Foltyn and Olsson is the framework this experiment adapts, replacing gender with country. Keep their under-two-points gender gap in mind as a yardstick. What this paper adds is the next slide.

The fourth line, for advice people: Foerster et al. (2017) find that advisor fixed effects explain more of client portfolios than client characteristics, and D’Acunto, Prabhala and Rossi (2019) and Rossi and Utkus (2024) find first-generation robo-advisors improving diversification. LLMs differ in being general-purpose and deployed in every language at once. Reuter and Schoar (2024) survey the market for advice.

Experimental design

Five seconds. The next three slides are the questions, the three prompt versions and the inference.

Each model is asked three questions

  • Portfolio. How to split the local equivalent of $50,000 between a broad stock-market index fund and government bonds over five years. Ages 30, 40 and 55.
  • Mortgage. Whether to invest savings in an index fund or prepay a 4% mortgage with twenty years left. Age 40.
  • Inflation. How to protect $50,000 earning 1% in a savings account against 3% inflation: the fraction to move and the primary instrument. Age 40.
  • Every answer. The same English instructions ask for JSON with the fraction and an explanation of at most 200 words. Fifty answers per cell, temperature 0.7, no system prompt.

The last line is what makes the explanation slides possible: every answer carries its own reasoning, in the language of the question. A JSON-first parser recovered a number from 99.9 percent of answers, and there were no refusals in 267,150.

The design in one slide. Fifteen countries are asked in the local language and six in English (U.S., UK, Canada, Australia, India, South Africa), so B-cross exists for the fifteen. The amount is the round local-currency equivalent of $50,000 in native number format.

The same prompt returns different answers each time at temperature 0.7, which is why every cell is asked 50 times. The instructions block with the JSON schema is English everywhere. Only the question is translated, by DeepL, then reviewed by native speakers in all fifteen languages.

Empirical specification

y_{imc} = \alpha_m + \gamma_c + \varepsilon_{imc}

  • Within model. y_{imc} is the equity share in repetition i by model m for country c. The model fixed effects \alpha_m make each \gamma_c a within-model contrast with the U.S.
  • Thirteen clusters. Standard errors cluster on 13 models
  • Multiple testing. The twenty country contrasts form one Holm family.
  • Pre-registered. Filed at OSF on 2 July 2026 and collected on 3–4 July. Primary cell: the portfolio question, Version B, age 40.

With G = 13, every p-value is a wild cluster bootstrap with Webb six-point weights and 9,999 replications.

Why Holm: like Bonferroni, it caps the chance of any false rejection among the twenty at 5 percent, whatever the correlation between the tests. It rejects at least as often, because only the smallest p-value faces the full 0.05/20 threshold. It is applied to the wild-bootstrap p-values. In the results, thirteen of the twenty contrasts are significant on their own and seven survive the correction.

The 4 and 6 points are the pre-registered detectable effects at the 5 percent threshold (5.5 and 7.6 at 80 percent power), computed before collection from an assumed spread across models. Realized precision varies by country: Mexico’s interval is narrow enough that a 2.7-point gap survives. The Netherlands is the marginal survivor, at Holm p = 0.049.

Collection details if asked: temperature 0.7, no system prompt, at most 4,000 tokens, truncated answers excluded. The whole grid of 267,150 queries ran in two days.

Results for country and language

Every country gap that survives correction is negative

Robustness

Point at the top: Brazil, Italy, Poland, France, Spain, the Netherlands, Mexico. Southern and central Europe plus Latin America. Every contrast that survives is negative, and the U.S. sits near the top of the band.

The yardstick for the rest of the talk: country effects are 3 to 11 points. That is small beside the 34-point model spread and beside what the stated investor does later, but larger than the under-two-points gender gaps of Foltyn and Olsson.

Whether these gaps are errors is the benchmark slide’s question. Brazil’s benchmark is low, so its gap may be a move toward theory. Germany: −4.0 points, interval −7.7 to −0.4, so it rejects on its own (p = 0.03) but not after Holm (p = 0.34), one of the hollow rings.

If asked about the rank of the covariance matrix: the joint test is the bootstrap Wald statistic. The asymptotic cluster-robust test can impose only 12 of the 20 restrictions with 13 clusters, and it also rejects, F(12, 12) = 34.26.

The advice bunches at round numbers and loosely follows 100 minus age

All 13,639 answers in the primary cell is a multiple of five, 85% are multiples of ten, with none below 10 or above 80.

The rule and its optimal tails: Love (2013). Exploratory, not pre-registered.

Age 30 is told 6.2 points more than age 40 and age 55 11.1 points less. The interaction with the pension replacement rate is an exact zero (ψ = −0.0002, p = 0.96): the same glide path in the most and least generous pension systems.

The deviations from the rule go toward conservatism at both ends, where Love (2013) puts the optimum above the rule. This is the same early-life conservatism Choukhmane et al. (2026) find a life-cycle model cannot rationalize. Exploratory, not pre-registered.

Explicit citations of the heuristic (“rule of thumb”, “100 minus age”) appear in 15 to 17 percent of U.S. and Indian explanations and in under 1 percent outside the English-prompt countries, while the allocations bunch identically.

The dollar-denominated stake

Two numbers, each about a point. Asking Lena’s question in German rather than English lowers the share by 1.3 points: significant, and an order of magnitude below the largest country effects. Naming the bAV, an MSCI World ETF and Bunds moves it by −0.7, not significant at 5 percent.

Why a small language effect is plausible: interpretability work finds that multilingual models solve tasks in a shared concept space that sits closer to English than to any other language (Wendler et al. 2024). It was not guaranteed: the same model often asserts different facts in different languages.

The United Kingdom is the pre-registered test of naming a country without changing the language: +0.7 points, interval −0.8 to +2.2, inside the ±5 margin registered for that test alone.

The country reference also changes the currency and the numeral of the amount, so “the country” here is the country, its currency and its number format together. The dollar-stake cell rules the numeral out for Germany, Japan and Sweden.

The explanations localize, but the number barely moves

How the text is coded

Start on Version B. Point at the two coloured rows: the U.S. at 18 percent and Germany at 63. Outside the U.S. the models reach for local instruments, taxes and pensions in half to nearly all of their explanations. So they know where the investor lives. Both panels share one 0 to 100 percent scale: the words range across the whole axis, the numbers sit in a narrow band.

The move: flip the switch to Version C, where the prompt names each country’s own pension, bonds and index fund. The hatched bars jump toward 100 percent, a 23-point rise, and the dots barely move, −0.7 points pooled. Dashed rings mark where each dot was. Brazil moves most, about 3 points, and the pooled effect is not significant at 5 percent.

The same country regression that finds a few points on the equity share finds up to 78 points on whether local context is named, so the design can see country variation where the answers contain it.

Objection, “the coder just finds the words you put in the prompt”: that is why Version B, which names no instruments, is the main test, and 47 to 96 percent there is unprompted. Under Version C the U.S. rate rises from 18 to 84 percent, so the coder does count U.S. instruments as local context.

Should advice differ across countries?

A way to think about the share across countries

For the investor with stake L, write the optimal share as: \theta^{*}_c = \min\left\{1,\; \overbrace{{\color{#C4452A}\frac{\mu_c - r_c}{\gamma_c\,\sigma_c^{2}}}}^{\text{Merton (1969)}} \times \underbrace{\left(1+\frac{B_c}{L}\right)}_{\text{Adjustment for other wealth}}\right\}

  • Risk aversion \gamma_c. is not stated in the prompt, but could be imputed by the model.
  • The equity premium \mu_c - r_c is the same in every currency under uncovered interest parity.
  • Volatility \sigma_c may or may not differ by country depending on currency hedging (Campbell and Viceira 2002).
  • Adjustment for other wealth. Counting the pension and human capital as safe wealth B_c beside the stake raises the share in stocks (Bodie et al. 1992; Cocco et al. 2005)

Answer depends whether the advice should take currency hedging into account

Start unhedged. Teal rings are what theory prescribes, vermilion dots what the models say. Theory spreads over thirty-two points, from 21 percent in Turkey to 53 in Korea. Lena’s euro adds almost nothing: theory gives her 52 and Emily 52, so the textbook reason for “less” from the hook is worth half a point for her. The advice spans twelve, and a regression of advice on theory gives a slope of 0.13, which rejects one and does not reject zero. The slope rests on Brazil and Turkey: dropping Brazil lowers it to 0.03, dropping Turkey raises it to 0.21, and every other leave-one-out slope is between 0.11 and 0.15.

The move: switch on the hedge. Every ring slides onto 52 percent, and the advice sits within six points of it in every country. This is true by construction, since the equity premium is common by uncovered interest parity and only the volatility differs, and that is the point: the whole spread in theory was exchange-rate risk.

What to say: under integrated markets the risky portfolio is the same world index everywhere, which rationalizes uniform advice. So the uniformity is defensible. The balance sheet is where theory and the advice part: counting pensions and human capital as safe wealth, theory is at the 100 percent corner in every country. Say that sentence out loud, it is the bridge to Part 4: does a stated balance sheet move the advice?

The country effects are only weakly related to national risk preferences and literacy

Risk taking from Falk et al. (2018). Literacy from Klapper et al. (2015).

A country one standard deviation more risk-tolerant gets under three points more equity, and the widest preference contrast in the panel, Japan to South Africa, predicts under four. South Africa, by far the most risk-tolerant, gets slightly less than the U.S.

This is descriptive: the prompt describes one investor, not a country mean, and imputing national risk-taking to her is an ecological inference an advisor need not make. Leave-one-out slopes stay positive and small. The two-stage bootstrap intervals exclude zero, and the wild bootstrap over countries does not.

What rule produces the advice?

What happens if you change the investor?

\theta^{*}_c = \min\left\{1,\; \overbrace{\frac{\mu_c - r_c}{\textcolor{#C4452A}{\gamma_c}\,\sigma_c^{2}}}^{\text{Merton (1969)}} \times \underbrace{\left(1+\frac{\textcolor{#C4452A}{B^{s}_c} + \textcolor{#C4452A}{B^{r}_c}}{L}\right)}_{\text{total wealth over the stake}} - \underbrace{\frac{\textcolor{#C4452A}{B^{r}_c}}{L}}_{\text{stocks held outside}}\right\}

  • Risk tolerance moves \gamma. “I am comfortable with substantial fluctuations…” against “I am uncomfortable with large fluctuations…”.
  • Pension generosity moves B^{s}. “A substantial pension…, projected to replace most of my pre-retirement income” against “only a small pension”.
  • The horizon is not in the formula. “Over a five-year horizon” becomes one year or twenty.

English Question 1, age 40, all 21 countries with their local amounts: Version B in the six English-prompt countries, B-cross in the other fifteen. Risk and pension crossed at five years (four cells), two horizon cells, about 13,600 answers per cell. Three pre-registered single tests.

I add one sentence about the investor to the English prompt and hold everything else fixed. Each sentence moves one term of the benchmark for the stake inside the balance sheet:

Theory predicts a response to tolerance, a response to the pension if the balance sheet counts, and none to the horizon.

The full sentences, if asked. Tolerant: “I am comfortable with substantial fluctuations in my portfolio value, including potential short-term losses, if it means higher expected returns over time.” Averse: “I am uncomfortable with large fluctuations in my portfolio value and would prefer to limit potential losses, even if it means lower expected returns.” Generous: “I will receive a substantial pension when I retire, projected to replace most of my pre-retirement income.” Limited: “I will receive only a small pension when I retire, covering a modest fraction of my pre-retirement income.” The statements are length-matched and institution-free, so acting on them needs no knowledge of local pension systems.

Objection, “mean reversion makes the horizon matter” (Campbell and Viceira 2002): that comes up on the comparison slide after the results.

After the next slide, if asked about heterogeneity: two of thirteen models are nearly horizon-invariant, while the most responsive moves 79 points between one year and twenty. The risk-tolerance response is positive for all thirteen models and within a 3-point band across the 21 countries.

The payoff for the horizon-or-pension bet. The bars grow in one by one. Say the numbers as they land. Stated risk tolerance: +46 points, from 30 to 78 percent. Horizon, twenty years against one: +40. Generous pension: −1.0 for the risk-averse investor, +2.6 for the risk-tolerant one. For scale, the largest country contrast is Brazil at −11, and language and instruments are about a point.

The teal marks are theory’s direction only. With constant relative risk aversion and independent returns the horizon is irrelevant. A lifecycle planner who counts pension wealth treats a generous pension as a bond and holds more stock.

Why English: the statements are added to the English prompt (Version B in the six English-speaking countries, B-cross elsewhere), so no translation stands between the sentence and the model.

Objection, “a one-year horizon is a spending date, and any planner holds bonds for that”: the twenty-year cell alone sits about 20 points above the five-year prompt with no added sentence, so the response is not only year-one liquidity.

Ross and Lo find the same dominance of risk tolerance with one vendor. New here: 13 models, 21 countries, and the contrast with a stated pension.

Stated risks cut the stock share sharply, stated safety barely raises it

Exploratory probes collected in August 2026, after the pre-registered results were examined.

Three pairs of sentences, each adding exposure or safety to the investor’s balance sheet.

Exposure cuts the share by up to points. The mirror statements of safety raise it by at most .

A currency hedge, the direct test of the benchmark slide, moves nothing.

A pension invested in stocks: −17 points, the right sign. A guaranteed inflation-linked annuity, which should crowd stock in: +4.8, barely significant. The stake is all my liquid wealth: −23. The stake is a third of my liquid wealth with twice as much in safe savings: +2.5, when the benchmark is already at its 100 percent corner. The hedge: nothing either way, for a statement that should matter most where currencies are volatile.

What to say: the models de-risk on stated exposure and let stated safety raise the share very little.

Objection, “all my liquid wealth means no emergency buffer, and cutting stock is standard advice”: agreed, and the benchmark has the same sign. The asymmetry is on the safe side, where a large cushion barely moves the number.

The wealth statements were asked in the U.S. prompt only, and a third wealth statement, half of liquid wealth, gives +1.4. Everything here is exploratory and post hoc, tested two-sided against the benchmark’s sign.

One minute, the callback to Part 2. Horizon and pension are both named in nearly every explanation, and only the horizon moves the number. The models mention the pension everywhere and decline to size the allocation to it. So counting mentions, as on the explanation slide, tells us what the models know, not what drives the advice.

For the risk-tolerant investor the generous pension raises the share by 2.6 points, an order of magnitude below the background-wealth adjustment.

Struck through and marked ✗: a prediction the measured effect contradicts. The comparison follows the popular-advice reading of Canner et al. (1997) and Choi (2022).

Walk the columns left to right, then the rows top to bottom. The first three rows are the pre-registered tests. The Merton planner gets risk tolerance and the pension right, because on the stake alone a pension does not enter, but it predicts no horizon effect, and the data show forty points. It also predicts no response to exposure outside the stake, and a hedge should raise its share where currencies are volatile. The lifecycle planner gets the sign of exposure right and is contradicted on the horizon, the pension and safety.

The questionnaire needs one addition that the probes reveal: a loss screen, the “can you afford to lose it” question. UK suitability guidance calls this capacity for loss, MiFID II the ability to bear losses. In practice it lowers the risk level that stated tolerance implies and does not raise it. That one-sided screen is the asymmetry of the last slide.

Say plainly that the last two rows are exploratory and that the loss screen was added after seeing them. The pre-registered rows alone already separate the questionnaire from both planners.

The questionnaire column is the one reading consistent with all five rows. It is a reading, and the paper says “fits”, not “proves”. The levels also fit a Merton planner with relative risk aversion between 2 and 5, so the levels alone cannot discriminate. The comparative statics can.

Objection, “the lifecycle benchmark sits at its 100 percent corner, so it predicts no response to safety either”: then it prescribes 100 percent with no statement at all, against advice near 54 percent. At the corner the lifecycle planner fails on the level, and off the corner it fails on the comparative statics.

Objection, “mean reversion rationalizes the horizon effect” (Campbell and Viceira 2002): yes, but a planner sophisticated enough to price return dynamics into forty points should not ignore pension wealth.

Bridge: the loss screen is where the implications start. Suitability rules oblige a human advisor to weigh the whole balance sheet, and the models weigh it only as a hazard.

Conclusions

  • Small, one-sided country effects. Country averages span 12 points and model averages 34.
  • The models know the country. Explanations fill with local context while the share barely moves, but on the mortgage question the country explains more of the variance than the model.
  • Uniformity is defensible, the balance sheet is not. A hedged world fund rationalizes one number everywhere, and theory and the advice part on the balance sheet.
  • A questionnaire, not a balance-sheet rule. A stated horizon moves the advice 40 points, a stated pension about 1, and stated safety raises it by at most 5.

What the design cannot show

Four claims, one breath each. The numbers behind them if someone wants them: seven significant contrasts, all negative, 3 to 11 points, and hedged theory at 52 percent everywhere. Version C raises local references by 23 points and moves the share by −0.7. On the mortgage question the country explains 27 percent of the variance and the model 15. Stated tolerance moves the advice 46 points, stated risks cut it by up to 23, stated safety raises it by at most 5.

Then the market-structure sentence: advice used to be a nationally segmented service, walled in by regulation, trust and language. The same handful of models now answer in every language at negligible marginal cost, outside the regulatory perimeter that bound human advisors. The error is common within a model, across all the households that ask it.

The design observes advice, not the computation behind it

  • No model internals. The shared-representation reading is an inference from behaviour, consistent with interpretability work, not a measurement.
  • A static benchmark. Closed form and one period. The one life-cycle treatment of LLM advice reaches the same corner (Choukhmane et al. 2026).
  • A fast-moving panel. The main collection is a two-day snapshot. Eleven weeks later DeepSeek V4 Flash gave 8.5 points more equity under an unchanged identifier, so replications need the collection date.
