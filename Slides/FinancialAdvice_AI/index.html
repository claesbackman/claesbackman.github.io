One Advisor for the Whole World?

Claes Bäckman

Leibniz Institute for Financial Research SAFE

Who is told to hold more stock?

The advice varies little with the country on the portfolio question, but more if the question requires local context

The model grid

Households everywhere now ask the same few models for financial advice

  • LLMs are becoming financial advisors. Over one third of individuals across OECD countries reported using AI tools (OECD 2026). The share of U.S. adults who use AI for financial advice roughly doubled between 2024 and 2025 (Financial Health Network 2025), and over half of U.K. adults use such tools to manage their money (Lloyds Banking Group 2025).
  • LLM advice comes from a handful of models. The models are trained overwhelmingly on U.S.-English text.
  • Advice markets are national. They reflect local norms, pension systems and regulation.

This paper

  • What should move the advice?
    → Models should respond to some parts, but should be invariant to others.
  • Does the advice differ across countries?
    → Barely on the portfolio question, more on mortgage and inflation questions.
  • What does the advice track?
    → No country characteristic on the portfolio question. Local interest rates on the mortgage question.
  • What rule produces the advice?
    → “Popular financial advice” (Canner, Mankiw, and Weil 1997; Choi 2022). Model ask the same questions asked in every country.

One advisor, following the same set of rules everywhere

Prior work mostly varies the investor

  • Choukhmane et al. (2026). U.S. households’ own prompts against a life-cycle model: moderately risk-averse advice in round numbers.
  • Ross and Lo (2026). Synthetic U.S. clients, stated risk tolerance carries most of the weight.
  • Foltyn and Olsson (2026). Gender gaps in financial advice in English are under two points.
  • Fieberg et al. (2025). 32 models and 64 hypothetical investors in China, Germany and the U.S. Risk exposure rises with stated risk tolerance, and the portfolios overweight domestic securities.
  • Fedyk et al. (2026). AI can capture the investment preferences and rationales of historically underrepresented investors.
  • Human and robo-advisors. Advisor fixed effects dominate client traits (Foerster et al. 2017), and robo-advice improves diversification (D’Acunto, Prabhala, and Rossi 2019; Rossi and Utkus 2024).

Experimental design

Each model is asked three questions

  • Portfolio. How to split the local equivalent of $50,000 between a broad stock-market index fund and government bonds over five years. Ages 30, 40 and 55.
  • Mortgage. Whether to invest savings in an index fund or prepay a 4% mortgage with twenty years left. Age 40.
  • Inflation. How to protect $50,000 earning 1% in a savings account against 3% inflation: the fraction to move and the primary instrument. Age 40.

Empirical specification

y_{imc} = \alpha_m + \gamma_c + \varepsilon_{imc}

  • Within model. y_{imc} is the equity share in repetition i by model m for country c. The model fixed effects \alpha_m make each \gamma_c a within-model contrast with the U.S.
  • Thirteen clusters. Standard errors cluster on 13 models, with wild cluster bootstrap (Roodman et al. 2019).
  • Multiple testing. Twenty country contrasts against the U.S. form one Holm family, which holds the chance of any false rejection at 5 percent.
  • Pre-registered. Filed at OSF on 2 July 2026 and collected on 3–4 July. Primary cell: the portfolio question, Version B, age 40.

What should move the advice?

A way to think about the share across countries

For the investor with stake W, write the optimal share as: \theta^{*}_c = \min\left\{1,\; \overbrace{{\color{#C4452A}\frac{\mu_c - r_c}{\gamma_c\,\sigma_c^{2}}}}^{\text{Merton (1969)}} \times \underbrace{\left(1+\frac{B^{s}_c + B^{r}_c}{W}\right)}_{\text{total wealth over the stake}} - \underbrace{\frac{B^{r}_c}{W}}_{\text{stocks held outside}}\right\}

  • Risk aversion \gamma_c. is not stated in the prompt, but could be imputed by the model.
  • The equity premium \mu_c - r_c is the same in every currency under uncovered interest parity, but models could use local returns.
  • Volatility \sigma_c may or may not differ by country depending on currency hedging (Campbell and Viceira 2002).
  • Wealth outside the stake. The investor targets a stock share of total wealth. Safe wealth elsewhere, B^{s}_c, such as a pension, raises the share of the stake in stocks. Stocks held elsewhere, B^{r}_c, lowers it (Bodie, Merton, and Samuelson 1992; Cocco, Gomes, and Maenhout 2005).

A tale of invariance and responsiveness

  • Invariance. What is outside the formula should not move the advice: the country name, the language of the question, the local names of the instruments, and the horizon.
  • Responsiveness. What is inside it should: risk aversion, currency risk, and wealth outside the stake.

What models respond to will also tell us what kind of advisor they are.

What happens if you change the investor?

\theta^{*}_c = \min\left\{1,\; \overbrace{\frac{\mu_c - r_c}{\textcolor{#C4452A}{\gamma_c}\,\sigma_c^{2}}}^{\text{Merton (1969)}} \times \underbrace{\left(1+\frac{\textcolor{#C4452A}{B^{s}_c} + \textcolor{#C4452A}{B^{r}_c}}{W}\right)}_{\text{total wealth over the stake}} - \underbrace{\frac{\textcolor{#C4452A}{B^{r}_c}}{W}}_{\text{stocks held outside}}\right\}

  • Risk tolerance moves \gamma. “I am comfortable with substantial fluctuations…” against “I am uncomfortable with large fluctuations…”.
  • Pension generosity moves B^{s}. “A substantial pension…, projected to replace most of my pre-retirement income” against “only a small pension”.
  • The horizon is not in the formula. “Over a five-year horizon” becomes one year or twenty.

Three more statements test the rest of the balance sheet

  • Pension composition moves B^{s} and B^{r}. “My pension is a guaranteed, inflation-linked annuity…” against “My pension is invested almost entirely in stocks…”.
  • The size of the stake moves B^{s}. The stake is all, about half, or about a third of the investor’s liquid wealth.
  • A currency hedge moves \sigma. “The stock fund I am considering is currency-hedged to my home currency…” against “…a global fund with no currency hedging…”.

Conclusions

  • The rules for assessing the investor are global. The models ask the same four questions in every country.
  • The recommendation localizes only where the question needs it.The mortgage and inflation advice tracks local interest rates.
  • The explanations localize most. They invoke local institutions even when the allocation barely changes, so the investor cannot tell from the answer whether the advice reflects her circumstances.
  • Common rules, common errors. Advice used to be a national service shaped by regulation, relationships and language. A handful of models now supply it everywhere, so the same recommendations and the same errors can reach households in otherwise distinct national markets.

What the design cannot show

One advisor for the whole world?

AI models globalizing not only the supply of financial advice but also the rules used to assess investors, while selectively adapting the recommendations to national financial environments.

Translated instructions, a PPP stake, the caller’s location and dropping any model leave the contrasts in place

  • Instructions translated too. The English format instructions rendered in the local language. The twenty country contrasts correlate at 0.93 with the main estimates, eight lie below the U.S. after Holm, and the range is 11 points against 12.

The design observes advice, not the computation behind it

  • No model internals. The shared-representation reading is an inference from behaviour, consistent with interpretability work, not a measurement.
  • A static benchmark. Closed form and one period. The one life-cycle treatment of LLM advice reaches the same corner (Choukhmane et al. 2026).
  • A fast-moving panel. The main collection is a two-day snapshot. Eleven weeks later DeepSeek V4 Flash gave 8.5 points more equity under an unchanged identifier, so replications need the collection date.
